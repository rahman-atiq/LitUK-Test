The Values & Principles of British Life

Foundations · applies to everyone living in the UK

British society is founded on fundamental values and principles which all those living in the UK should respect and support. They are rooted in history, traditions, law, customs and expectations. There is no place in British society for extremism or intolerance.

5

5

0

The 5 fundamental principles

  • 1 · Democracy
  • 2 · The rule of law
  • 3 · Individual liberty
  • 4 · Tolerance of those with different faiths and beliefs
  • 5 · Participation in community life

Your 5 responsibilities

  • Respect and obey the law
  • Respect the rights of others, including their right to their own opinions
  • Treat others with fairness
  • Look after yourself and your family
  • Look after the area in which you live, and the environment

In return, the UK offers

  • Freedom of belief and religion
  • Freedom of speech
  • Freedom from unfair discrimination
  • A right to a fair trial
  • A right to join in the election of a government
The citizenship pledge — recited by new citizens at their ceremony
“I will give my loyalty to the United Kingdom and respect its rights and freedoms. I will uphold its democratic values. I will observe its laws faithfully and fulfil my duties and obligations as a British citizen.”

The values and responsibilities above are a direct expansion of this pledge — learn them as one unit.

Memory hook The rule of fives — “D-R-I-T-P” Democracy, Rule of law, Individual liberty, Tolerance, Participation. Five principles, matched by five duties you owe and five freedoms you get. Three lists of five: fifteen facts, one chapter.
Exam trap “Participation in community life” is a fundamental principle — it's the one candidates forget. And the freedoms list includes the right to a fair trial and the right to join in electing a government — both count as “freedoms offered by the UK”, not just legal details.

Becoming a Permanent Resident

Requirements · routes · the 2013 changes

To apply for permanent residence or citizenship you must be able to speak and read English and have a good understanding of life in the UK. Since January 2013 there are two ways to prove it — and from October 2013 the rules tightened.

Jan 2013
two routes introduced
Oct 2013
test + B1 English both required
Entry 3
ESOL level built into the test
Tier 1 & 2
must take the LitUK test

Route A · The Life in the UK test

  • Questions are written to require English at ESOL Entry Level 3 — so passing means no separate English test is needed.
  • Mandatory for work-visa holders on Tier 1 and Tier 2 of the points-based system.

Route B · ESOL course with citizenship

  • For applicants whose English is below ESOL Entry Level 3.
  • A course that improves your English and teaches you about life in the UK — ending with a test/assessment.

From October 2013 — settlement needs both

  • Pass the Life in the UK test, and
  • Provide evidence of speaking and listening in English at B1 of the Common European Framework of Reference (CEFR) — the equivalent of ESOL Entry Level 3.

Applying

  • After passing, you send in application forms and supporting evidence; what you need depends on your circumstances.
  • A fee applies, and it varies by application type.
  • All forms and the list of fees are on the UK Border Agency website — www.ukba.homeoffice.gov.uk.
  • Requirements can change — always check the UK Border Agency website before applying.
Memory hook B1 and Entry Level 3 are the same rung Two ladders, one height: B1 (CEFR) is the European name, ESOL Entry Level 3 is the British name. January 2013 gave you a choice of routes; October 2013 said “test AND B1, both”.
Exam trap The ESOL route is not a shortcut past knowledge — the course covers English AND life in the UK, and still ends in a test. And note who has no choice: Tier 1 and Tier 2 visa holders must take the Life in the UK test itself.

Taking the Life in the UK Test

Format · booking · what to bring

The test asks about itself surprisingly often. Its format, its languages, its booking rules and its ID requirements are all fair game — memorise the logistics like any other fact.

24
questions in the test
~60
registered test centres in the UK
3
languages: English, Welsh, Scottish Gaelic
2
documents: ID + proof of address

The format

  • The test consists of 24 questions about important aspects of life in the UK.
  • Questions are based on ALL parts of the handbook — including the introduction.

Languages

  • Usually taken in English.
  • Special arrangements can be made to take it in Welsh or Scottish Gaelic.

Where

  • Only at a registered and approved Life in the UK test centre — there are about 60 around the UK.
  • The UK Border Agency only accepts certificates from registered centres — never take it anywhere else.
  • Isle of Man and Channel Islands residents: special arrangements apply.

Booking & the day itself

  • Booking is online only, at www.lifeintheuktest.gov.uk — read the instructions and enter your details correctly.
  • Bring identification and proof of your address. Without them, you will not be allowed to take the test.
Memory hook 24 · 60 · 3 · 2 24 questions, 60 centres, 3 possible languages, 2 documents in your pocket. One string of four numbers covers the whole section.
Exam trap Questions come from the whole handbook including the introduction — not just the “testable chapters”. Booking is online only — a question offering phone or in-person booking is lying to you.
