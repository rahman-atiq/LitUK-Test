Follow the Power

Chapter 5 is the counting chapter — member counts, jury sizes, membership numbers — and it carries more "who does what?" questions than any other. So don't study it as a civics textbook. Study it as a relay race: power starts at the Crown, passes to Parliament, to the Prime Minister, out to the nations and the world, down through the courts — and the final runner, at the very end of the chain, is you. Act I · The constitution

The Invisible Rulebook

Britain is governed by a constitution nobody ever wrote down — because nobody ever had to start from scratch. At its top sits a monarch who reigns but does not rule, and below her two chambers: one elected, one appointed, both centuries old. This act is the who-appoints-whom act. Get the arrows right and it's free marks.

📜

The rulebookA constitution you cannot photocopy

A constitution is the set of principles by which a country is governed. Britain's is unwritten — not collected in a single document — and the reason is Chapter 3 in one sentence: the country avoided revolution, so its institutions were never demolished and redrafted; they just developed gradually over centuries. The parts of this uncodified machine: the monarchy, Parliament (Commons and Lords), the Prime Minister, the cabinet, the judiciary, the police, the civil service, local government — plus the devolved governments we'll visit in Act II.

⚠️ Test trap: it's unwritten because Britain never had a revolution — not because nobody got round to writing it. The handbook's exact logic; the test's exact question.

And at the very top of this unwritten order sits its oldest institution — the Crown…

👑

Since 1952The Queen — head of state, not head of government

Queen Elizabeth II has reigned since her father's death in 1952 — her Diamond Jubilee (60 years) came in 2012. She is head of state of the UK and of many Commonwealth countries, married to Prince Philip, Duke of Edinburgh, with Prince Charles, the Prince of Wales as heir. But this is a constitutional monarchy: the monarch does not rule. She appoints the government the people have elected, inviting the leader who commands most MPs — or a coalition — to become Prime Minister. Meeting the PM regularly, she may "advise, warn and encourage" — three verbs, and none of them is decide.

🧠 Pin it: the monarch's three verbs — advise, warn, encourage. Any answer that gives the Queen the word "decide" or "policy" is bait.

What she does do is open the machine each year — with a speech written by someone else…

🎤

The ceremoniesWhat the Crown actually does all day

The monarch opens Parliament each year with the Queen's Speech — a summary of the government's plans, not her own. Every Act of Parliament is made in her name. She receives foreign ambassadors, hosts visiting heads of state, and makes state visits abroad — the focus for national identity and pride, the fixed point while governments come and go. Two texts travel with the Crown: the National Anthem ("God save our gracious Queen!…") and the citizenship oath sworn by new citizens — which we'll save for the very last scene of this story, because it's yours.

The Crown appoints but doesn't choose. The choosing happens in an elected chamber down the river — where the real power sits…

🏛️

The CommonsThe elected chamber — and its referee

The House of Commons is the more important chamber for one reason: its members are democratically elected. Each MP represents a parliamentary constituency; the PM and almost all cabinet ministers are MPs. Their job: represent everyone in the constituency, help create new laws, scrutinise and question the government, debate the big issues. Keeping order is the Speaker — an MP, yet strictly neutral, chosen by fellow MPs in a secret ballot. The Speaker chairs debates, guarantees the opposition its debate time, and represents Parliament on ceremonial occasions.

⚠️ Test trap: the Speaker is chosen by secret ballot of MPs — not appointed by the PM, not by the Queen. Neutral referee, elected by the players.

Across the lobby sits the other chamber — unelected, unhurried, and twice reformed in ways the test loves to date…

🦁

The Lords1958 gives life · 1999 takes inheritance

Members of the House of Lords are peers — not elected, no constituency. Until 1958 the house belonged to hereditary title-holders, senior judges and Church of England bishops. Then two reforms, two dates: in 1958, life peers arrive — nominated by the Prime Minister (with other party leaders and an independent Appointments Commission), appointed by the monarch, for life. In 1999, hereditary peers lose their automatic right to attend, keeping only a few elected representatives of their own. The Lords' role: more independent than the Commons — proposing amendments, checking laws, holding government to account with specialist knowledge. The Commons can overrule the Lords — but rarely does.

🧠 Pin it: 1958 gives life, 1999 takes inheritance. Two beats of Lords reform — nominate-PM, appoint-monarch in the middle.

Elected or appointed, they all answer to one mechanism in the end — the election that put the Commons there…

🗳️

ElectionsFirst past the post, every five years at most

General elections happen at least every five years. The voting system is first past the post: most votes in the constituency wins the seat; the party with a majority of constituencies forms the government — no majority may mean a coalition. If an MP dies or resigns, a by-election fills the seat. One deliberate contrast: European Parliament elections use proportional representation, electing MEPs every five years.

⚠️ Test trap: first past the post elects MPs; proportional representation elects MEPs (and, as you'll see, the devolved bodies). Two systems, never swap them.

And whoever wins that race gets the keys to a famous black door — because the monarch appoints, but the voters have already chosen…

Previously: an unwritten rulebook, a Queen with three verbs — advise, warn, encourage — a Commons with a neutral Speaker, Lords reformed in 1958 and 1999, and elections by first past the post, five years at most.

Act II · The government

The Engine Room

Behind the black door: one Prime Minister, about twenty ministers, a permanent civil service — and, since 1997, three more parliaments sharing the work. Memorise the devolution phone number now: 60 – 129 – 108. This act dials it.

🚪

Downing StreetThe Prime Minister

The Prime Minister is the leader of the party in power. The job comes with 10 Downing Street, a country house — Chequers — and the two levers that matter: appointing the cabinet and control of important public appointments. How does a PM leave? Their own party MPs can change leader, or they resign — usually after losing an election. Nobody needs to storm anything; the unwritten rulebook handles it quietly.

One person can't run five departments before lunch — so the PM deals out portfolios around a table…

🪑

The cabinetTwenty chairs, weekly

The cabinet: about 20 senior MPs made ministers in charge of departments, meeting weekly — big decisions still often need Parliament. Learn the three headline jobs as a who-does-what: the Chancellor of the Exchequer — the economy; the Home Secretary — crime, policing and immigration; the Foreign Secretary — relations with foreign countries. Other Secretaries of State take education, health and defence, propped up by Ministers of State and Parliamentary Under-Secretaries.

⚠️ Test trap: immigration belongs to the Home Secretary, not the Foreign Secretary — "foreign" is the word doing the baiting. Economy = Chancellor.

And facing them across the chamber, a full-time rival government-in-waiting…

⚔️

The oppositionThe shadow across the aisle

The second-largest party in the Commons is the opposition. Its leader challenges the PM at Prime Minister's Questions every sitting week and appoints a shadow cabinet — a mirror minister for every real one. The wider cast: the main parties are Conservative, Labour and the Liberal Democrats, plus national parties for Scottish, Welsh and Northern Irish interests. Outside Parliament, pressure and lobby groups push policy from the wings — the handbook names the CBI (business), Greenpeace (environment) and Liberty (human rights).

Ministers come and go with elections — but somebody has to actually run the machine on Monday morning…

🗂️

The permanent machineCivil servants and the town hall

Civil servants deliver and develop policy, are accountable to ministers, chosen on merit, and — crucially — politically neutral: their stated values are integrity, honesty, objectivity, impartiality. Governments change; they stay. Below Westminster, local authorities: elected councils funded by central government and local taxes, some areas with both district and county councils, many with a ceremonial mayor. London has 33 local authorities plus the Greater London Authority and Mayor of London. Council elections are held in May.

🧠 Pin it: civil service = merit + neutral; councils = May elections; London = 33 + the GLA. Three small facts, three easy marks.

And in 1997 Westminster did something Chapter 3 spent centuries building up to — it started handing power back to the nations…

🏴

Since 1997Devolution — the phone number

Devolution begins in 1997. Westminster keeps D-F-I-T-S — defence, foreign affairs, immigration, taxation, social security — and the nations run much of the rest, including education. Wales: the National Assembly in Cardiff, 60 AMs, elected every four years by proportional representation, working in Welsh and English; since 2011 it can pass laws in 20 areas without Westminster's agreement. Scotland: the Scottish Parliament at Holyrood (est. 1999), 129 MSPs, legislating on everything not reserved — including civil and criminal law — and it can raise taxes. Northern Ireland: an earlier parliament (1922) was abolished in 1972; today's Assembly at Stormont followed the Belfast (Good Friday) Agreement, 1998 — 108 MLAs governing by power-sharing. Westminster can suspend it; it has run continuously since 2007.

🧠 Pin it: the devolution phone number — 60 – 129 – 108 (Wales, Scotland, NI) — and the reserved list D-F-I-T-S. Among the three, only Scotland can raise taxes.

All this machinery runs in the open — printed, broadcast, and answerable to the one register with your name on it…

📋

The open machineHansard, the register, and who gets a vote

Proceedings are broadcast on TV and published in Hansard. The UK has a free press — newspapers may campaign — but radio and TV coverage must be balanced, giving rival viewpoints equal time. Now the rules that reach you: the voting age is 18, set in 1969. UK-born and naturalised citizens vote in all elections, and so do resident Commonwealth and Irish citizens; resident EU citizens vote in all elections EXCEPT general elections. To vote at all you must be on the electoral register — a form visits every household each September or October (Northern Ireland registers individually). Polling stations — polling places in Scotland — open 7 am–10 pm; NI voters need photo ID. To stand for office: 18+, a UK, Irish or Commonwealth citizen — with exceptions for armed forces, civil servants and certain convictions. And you can simply visit: public galleries in both Houses (write to your MP or queue), plus Holyrood, the Senedd and Stormont.

⚠️ Test trap: EU citizens: everything but general elections. Commonwealth & Irish: everything. This single distinction is one of the most-tested lines in the book.

A state this organised doesn't stop at its own coastline — it joins clubs. Five of them, each with a number on the door…

Previously: the PM behind the black door, ~20 cabinet chairs, a neutral civil service, and the devolution phone number — 60 · 129 · 108 — with D-F-I-T-S kept at Westminster.

Act III · The world stage

The Island and the World

This whole act is five organisations and their numbers. Learn the descending staircase — 190+ → 54 → 47 → 27 → 15 → 5 — and every likely question is already answered. One breath, six numbers.

🌐

Club № 1The Commonwealth — 54, with the Queen at its head

The family the Empire left behind: 54 member states, with the Queen at its head. Membership is voluntary; the club has no power over its members — the strongest thing it can do is suspend membership. What binds it is a creed you'll recognise from this whole chapter: democracy, good government and the rule of law.

One club looks back at the Empire; the next was built next door, out of a war — and Britain arrived sixteen years late…

🇪🇺

Club № 2The EU — Rome 1957, Britain 1973

On 25 March 1957, six countries — Belgium, France, Germany, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands — sign the Treaty of Rome and create the EEC. The UK stays out, then joins in 1973. In the handbook's edition the club has 27 member states, and EU law — directives, regulations, framework decisions — is legally binding in the UK and every member state.

🧠 Pin it: Rome 1957 without Britain; Britain 1973, sixteen years late. Six founders — the Benelux three plus France, Germany, Italy.

Now the club everyone confuses with the EU — different building, different job, nearly twice the members…

⚖️

Club № 3The Council of Europe — 47, and it is NOT the EU

The Council of Europe has 47 members and one obsession: protecting human rights. It cannot make laws — it draws up conventions and charters, most famously the European Convention on Human Rights. Hold that thought: the ECHR returns in Act V as the backbone of your own rights.

⚠️ Test trap: Council of Europe ≠ EU. 47 members, makes no laws, wrote the ECHR. The EU has 27 and its law binds. The test swaps these two constantly.

And above them all, the biggest club of all — built after the biggest war of all…

🕊️

Clubs № 4 & 5The UN and NATO — the peacekeepers

The United Nations, founded after the Second World War to prevent war and promote peace, counts 190+ members. Its Security Council seats 15, and the UK is one of just 5 permanent members. Finally NATO — the North Atlantic Treaty Organization — where members promise to defend each other if attacked and keep the peace. Now read the staircase down in one breath: 190+ UN → 54 Commonwealth → 47 Council of Europe → 27 EU → 15 Security Council → 5 permanent members.

🧠 Pin it: the descending staircase — 190+ · 54 · 47 · 27 · 15 · 5. Biggest to smallest, one breath, every membership question covered.

Treaties promise peace abroad. At home, peace is kept by something older and closer: the law — and it applies to absolutely everyone…

Previously: five clubs on a staircase — 190+ UN, 54 Commonwealth, 47 Council of Europe, 27 EU, 15 Security Council seats, 5 permanent members, Britain among them.

Act IV · Respecting the law

The Two Lanes of the Law

Criminal law versus civil law, magistrates versus Crown Court, 12 jurors versus 15 — this act lives in the gap between pairs. And one rule of thumb saves you eight facts: Scotland does everything a bit differently.

🛣️

The forkCriminal lane, civil lane

The law applies equally to everyone — police and government included — and knowing it is a core duty of every resident. It splits into two lanes. Criminal law — offences investigated by police and punished by courts: carrying a weapon of any kind, even for self-defence; buying or selling drugs; racial crime; selling tobacco or alcohol to under-18s; smoking in enclosed public places; drinking in alcohol-free zones. Civil law — disputes between people: housing (landlord v tenant), consumer rights (faulty goods), employment (wages, dismissal, discrimination), and debt.

⚠️ Test trap: carrying a weapon is criminal "even if it is for self-defence" — the handbook's exact bait, word for word. There is no self-defence exception.

Someone has to police the criminal lane — and in Britain, the police answer to the law, not to the government…

👮

The policeProtect, keep, prevent — and answer for it

Three duties: protect life and property, keep the peace, prevent and detect crime. Forces are independent of government, led by Chief Constables — who are appointed by Police and Crime Commissioners (PCCs), elected by the public since November 2012 in England and Wales to set local priorities and budgets. Officers must obey the law themselves: no misuse of authority, no false statements, no rudeness, no discrimination. PCSOs patrol and support at crime scenes. Complaints go to the station or Chief Constable — or up to the Independent Police Complaints Commission (E&W), Police Complaints Commissioner (Scotland), Police Ombudsman (NI). And the threats the handbook names: Al Qa'ida-style groups and Northern Ireland-related terrorism — report extremist recruiters to your local police.

⚠️ Test trap: the arrow points down — elected PCCs appoint Chief Constables, never the other way round. First PCC elections: November 2012.

The police catch; they don't judge. Judging belongs to a bench the government cannot touch…

⚖️

The criminal courtsUnpaid magistrates, twelve jurors — fifteen in Scotland

Judges interpret the law and keep trials fair — the government cannot interfere; if the government itself acts illegally, it must change its policies or ask Parliament to change the law. Minor crime goes to Magistrates' Courts (England, Wales, NI) or Justice of the Peace Courts (Scotland) — magistrates and JPs are unpaid and need no legal qualifications, except in Northern Ireland, where District Judges are paid and legally qualified. Serious crime goes to the Crown Court — in Scotland, the Sheriff Court — with judge and jury: 12 jurors in England, Wales and NI, 15 in Scotland. Verdicts: guilty or not guilty — and Scotland alone keeps a third, "not proven". Jury service is compulsory for those summoned, unless ineligible or excused for good reason.

🧠 Pin it: Scotland does everything a bit differently — JP Courts, Sheriff Court, 15 jurors, three verdicts. One rule of thumb, four facts banked.

And when the accused is barely more than a child, the courtroom changes shape entirely…

🧒

The youngYouth Court — doors closed, names sealed

Accused aged 10–17 go to the Youth Court, before up to three specially trained magistrates. Parents must attend, the public is excluded, and the young person cannot be named or photographed in the media. Scotland, naturally, does it differently — the Children's Hearings system — while Northern Ireland uses youth conferencing.

That's the criminal lane end to end. The civil lane has its own courts — and a small-claims shortcut with two price tags…

💼

The civil courtsCounty Courts and the small-claims shortcut

Civil disputes go to County Courts — in Scotland, the Sheriff Court again — with the biggest cases rising to the High Court (Scotland: the Court of Session). For modest sums there's the small claims procedure: informal, in an ordinary room before a judge, for claims under £5,000 in England and Wales and under £3,000 in Scotland and Northern Ireland — claims can even start at Money Claims Online. For everything heavier: solicitors, trained lawyers whose fees usually follow time spent — so ask the price before the clock starts.

🧠 Pin it: small claims — £5,000 E&W, £3,000 Scotland & NI. England's limit is the bigger one; Scotland differs again, right on brand.

Courts protect you from others. But who protects you from power itself? For that, Britain spent 300 years forging you a shield…

Previously: two lanes of law, police under elected PCCs since November 2012, unpaid magistrates, juries of 12 — 15 in Scotland, with "not proven" — and a £5,000 / £3,000 small-claims split.

Act V · Fundamental principles

Your Shield

Three dates forge it — 1689, 1950, 1998 — and then the shield follows you into ordinary life: equal treatment, protection at home, the taxes you'll pay, the licence you'll drive on. The rights chapter, worn like armour.

🛡️

Three datesForging the shield

Britain's rights tradition runs from Magna Carta through the Habeas Corpus Act to the Bill of Rights of 1689 — Chapter 3's greatest hits. Then the modern strokes: British experts help draft the European Convention on Human Rights, which the UK signs in 1950; and the Human Rights Act 1998 writes the Convention into UK law. The rights to list: life; no torture; no slavery or forced labour; liberty and security; a fair trial; freedom of thought, conscience and religion; freedom of expression.

🧠 Pin it: the rights timeline — 1689 starts it, 1950 signs it, 1998 brings it home. Three hammer-blows, one shield.

A shield is only real if it covers everyday life — the workplace, the home, the marriage…

🤝

Equal & protectedThe shield in daily life

Discrimination is unlawful on grounds of age, disability, sex, pregnancy and maternity, race, religion or belief, sexuality or marital status — with help from the Citizens Advice Bureau and the Equality and Human Rights Commission. Domestic violence is a serious crime; the 24-hour National Domestic Violence Freephone Helpline is 0808 2000 247. FGM is illegal — including taking someone abroad for it. And a distinction the test adores: arranged marriage, where both parties agree, is perfectly acceptable; forced marriage, without free consent, is a crime — Forced Marriage Protection Orders arrived in 2008 (Scotland: November 2011), and breaching one can mean two years in prison.

⚠️ Test trap: arranged ≠ forced. Arranged = both agree = legal. Forced = no free consent = criminal. One word, one mark, don't drop it.

Rights come with a membership fee, collected by a department with three initials…

🧾

The feeIncome tax and National Insurance — different taxes, different jobs

Income tax applies to wages, self-employed profits, taxable benefits, pensions and savings — and it pays for roads, education, police and the armed forces. Employees pay through PAYE — Pay As You Earn; the self-employed file self-assessment with HMRC. Alongside it, almost everyone in paid work also pays National Insurance Contributions, which fund state benefits and the NHS — including the state retirement pension; too few contributions can mean no Jobseeker's Allowance or an incomplete pension. Your National Insurance number arrives just before your 16th birthday; non-UK nationals get one via the DWP to prove identity and the right to work.

⚠️ Test trap: income tax builds roads and schools; National Insurance funds the NHS and pension. Two taxes, two jobs — the test lives in the gap.

And the last piece of armour is laminated — the driving licence, with an age ladder all of its own…

🚗

The roadThe driving ladder

Drive a car or motorcycle at 17, a moped at 16 — after a theory test (multiple choice + hazard perception) and a practical test. The licence runs to age 70, then renews every three years. Northern Ireland's newly qualified drivers show an R (restricted) plate for one year. Foreign licences: EU, Iceland, Liechtenstein or Norway — valid as long as the licence is; all others — 12 months, then you need a UK licence. The car itself must be registered with the DVLA, carry road tax and insurance — driving uninsured is a serious criminal offence — plus an annual MOT once it's over three years old.

🧠 Pin it: the driving ladder — 16 moped → 17 car → 70 renewals begin → every 3 years after. And the MOT clock starts at car age 3.

Registered, taxed, insured, protected — the system has given you everything it has. Which leaves one last question: what do you give back?

Previously: a shield forged in 1689, 1950 and 1998 — equal treatment, arranged-not-forced, PAYE and National Insurance, and a driving ladder from 16 to 70.

Act VI · Your role

Your Turn

The baton reaches the last runner. The chapter — and the whole handbook — ends with what Britain asks of you: shared values, a jury summons, a school hall, a pint of blood, and one short oath. The final scene of this story is you.

🏡

The valuesThe unwritten contract (this one's also unwritten)

The shared values, in the handbook's own cadence: obey and respect the law; be aware of and respect others' rights; treat others with fairness; behave responsibly; help and protect your family; respect and preserve the environment; treat everyone equally; work to support yourself and your family; help others; vote. And the domestic version — being a good neighbour: introduce yourself, respect privacy, keep the noise down, tend your garden, and put the bins out on collection days only.

Fine words — but the system makes them concrete. Sometimes it even sends you a letter with a seat number on it…

📬

The summonsJury duty, school halls, party colours

Remember registering to vote in Act II? The electoral register has a second job: anyone on it aged 18–70 can be randomly selected for jury service — the same juries you met in Act IV. In schools, parents and volunteers listen to children read, help in class, and fundraise through the PTA; at 18+ — with no upper age limit — you can become a school governor (in Scotland, a school board member), setting strategic direction, ensuring accountability and monitoring performance. The politically minded can canvass for a party or stand for office. And for teenagers there's the National Citizen Service — ages 16–17, outdoor challenges and community projects.

🧠 Pin it: three age brackets carry this act — jury 18–70 · governor 18 with no ceiling · NCS 16–17. Jury duty is the only one that retires at 70.

Some gifts cost time, some cost an hour and a needle…

🩸

The givingBlood, kidneys, and nine charities

Giving blood takes about an hour. Organ donation runs through the NHS register — and a living donor can give a kidney. You can volunteer with hospitals and youth projects, or serve as a special constable or lay magistrate (the unpaid bench from Act IV — now you know where they come from). Match the handbook's charities to their causes: NSPCC — children; Age UK — older people; Crisis and Shelter — homelessness; Cancer Research UK — cancer; PDSA — animals; Friends of the Earth — environment; the British Red Cross and the National Trust — the same Trust with the 61,000 volunteers from Chapter 4. And the greenest giving is habit: recycle, shop locally, walk or take public transport.

⚠️ Test trap: charity-to-cause is a free mark if you've matched them once — PDSA is animals, Shelter/Crisis are homelessness, NSPCC is children. Don't improvise on test day.

Which brings the baton to its final holder. One room, one flag, one short sentence — and the whole system you just scrolled through stands behind it…

🤲

The last sceneThe ceremony — where this story was always heading

At the citizenship ceremony, new citizens swear an oath or affirm loyalty: "…I will be faithful and bear true allegiance to Her Majesty Queen Elizabeth the Second, her Heirs and Successors, according to law." Trace the sentence back up the chain and you've revised the whole chapter: the Queen from Act I, according to law from Act IV, the rights that protect the promise from Act V, and the vote, jury seat and neighbourhood you're promising into from Acts II and VI. The power you've been following all chapter ends its journey in the same place the handbook does: in your hands.

🧠 Pin it: the anthem and the oath both get quoted on the test — recognise the first verse of God Save the Queen and the oath's "faithful and bear true allegiance" line.

And that's the machine, top to bottom — Crown to citizen. All that's left is to prove you can hold the numbers…

Epilogue

The whole system in one breath

An unwritten constitution (no revolution, no rewrite) puts a Queen on top — 1952, three verbs: advise, warn, encourage — who appoints but never decides. Below her, an elected Commons with a secret-ballot Speaker outranks Lords reformed twice (1958 gives life, 1999 takes inheritance), chosen first past the post at least every 5 years. The winner takes No. 10, deals ~20 cabinet chairs — Chancellor economy, Home Secretary immigration — shadowed at PMQs, served by a neutral civil service, and since 1997 shares power down the phone number 60 · 129 · 108 (Wales · Holyrood 1999 · Stormont's Good Friday 1998), keeping only D-F-I-T-S. Abroad, the memberships descend a staircase: 190+ · 54 · 47 · 27 · 15 · 5. At home the law runs in two lanes — criminal and civil — policed by forces under elected PCCs (Nov 2012), judged by unpaid magistrates and juries of 12 (15 in Scotland, plus "not proven"), with small claims capped at £5,000 / £3,000. Your shield was forged 1689 → 1950 → 1998; your dues are PAYE and National Insurance; your licence runs 17 to 70. And the chain ends with you: jury 18–70, governor 18+, NCS 16–17, blood in an hour — and one short oath, sworn according to law.
