Your First Year

Chapter 4 looks like trivia — hundreds of names, dates and percentages with no plot to hang them on. So we're giving it one: you. You've just landed in the UK, and this is the diary of your first year here — the money you change on day one, the saints and festivals that mark the calendar, the summer of sport, the nights out, the days off. Live the year once, and the facts stop being a list. They become things that happened to you.

Act I · Week one

Arrivals

Passport stamped, suitcase heavy, everything unfamiliar. Your first week is spent learning the basics everyone else was born knowing: four capitals, one currency, three extra languages — and a chant of four numbers that answers more test questions than any king or queen: 84 – 8 – 5 – 3.

🛬

Day 1Touchdown — four nations, four capitals

The plane drops through the cloud and there it is: one state, four nations, and you need all four capitals cold. London is the capital of the UK (and of England), Edinburgh of Scotland, Cardiff of Wales, Belfast of Northern Ireland. Four cities, four flags on the arrivals board. Say them once out loud in the taxi queue — the test will ask, and it will try to sell you Glasgow.

The taxi won't take euros — first stop, the cash machine…

💷

Day 1, an hour laterThe money in your pocket

The machine deals you the pound sterling (£) — 100 pence to the pound. The coins come in eight sizes: 1p, 2p, 5p, 10p, 20p, 50p, £1, £2. The notes come in four: £5, £10, £20, £50 — and that's where the ladder stops. There is no handbook £100 note, however hard the test winks at you.

⚠️ Test trap: the note ladder ends at £50. Eight coins, four notes — count them on your fingers once and it sticks.

Cash in hand, you board the train north — and the station signs start speaking in tongues…

🗣️

Day 3The island speaks back

English isn't travelling alone. In Wales the signs are bilingual because Welsh is spoken widely and taught in schools and universities. In the Scottish Highlands and Islands you'll hear Gaelic; in Northern Ireland, its cousin Irish Gaelic. Three Celtic survivors, each pinned to its own corner of the map — the same corners the Romans never fully tamed, if you remember Chapter 3.

🧠 Pin it: Welsh = schools (it's the one formally taught), Gaelic = Highlands & Islands, Irish Gaelic = NI. One language, one landmark each.

And the train keeps stopping — at city after city you're supposed to be able to place…

🏙️

Day 5The city roll-call

Out the window they roll past. England gets the long list: Birmingham, Liverpool, Leeds, Sheffield, Bristol, Manchester, Bradford, Newcastle upon Tyne, Plymouth, Southampton, Norwich. Wales adds Swansea and Newport; Scotland adds Glasgow, Dundee and Aberdeen; Northern Ireland's listed city is Belfast itself.

⚠️ Test trap: Glasgow is Scotland's biggest listed city — but Edinburgh is the capital. The test loves this swap more than any other in the chapter.

Cities are where the people are — so who exactly shares this island with you?

🧑‍🤝‍🧑

Week 2The people — a chant of four numbers

Here's the single most valuable chant in Chapter 4: 84 – 8 – 5 – 3. That's the share of the population living in England (84%), Scotland (8%), Wales (5%) and Northern Ireland (under 3%). And the country is a nation of arrivals like you: nearly 10% of people have a parent or grandparent born outside the UK. As for equality — discrimination is against the law, women are about half the workforce, girls leave school with better average qualifications than boys, and more women than men study at university.

🧠 Pin it: 84–8–5–3 — say it like a PIN code. Wales beats Northern Ireland (5% vs under 3%); don't let the little ones swap.

And the crowd around you wasn't always this size — the register tells a growth story worth one more look…

📈

Week 2, the libraryThe population curve

In 1600 there were just over 4 million people; in 1700, 5 million. Then the Victorian rocket: 1801 = 8 million, 1851 = 20 million, 1901 = 40 million — the population more than doubles twice in one century, the same century Chapter 3's factories were roaring. After that it climbs gently: 50m in 1951, 57m in 1998, just under 60m in 2005, just over 62m in 2010.

🧠 Pin it: the 19th century is 8 → 20 → 40. Everything before is single digits, everything after crawls. One doubling curve, three numbers.
Field notes — the full curve, decade stones

1600 · just over 4m — 1700 · 5m — 1801 · 8m — 1851 · 20m — 1901 · 40m — 1951 · 50m — 1998 · 57m — 2005 · just under 60m — 2010 · just over 62m.

Notice the phrasing the handbook uses: "just over" 4m and 62m, "just under" 60m — the test sometimes quotes it word for word.

By the end of week two you have money, a map and the measure of the crowd. Then Sunday comes — and the bells start…

Previously: you landed, learned the chant — 84–8–5–3 — pocketed a currency that stops at £50, and placed the cities. Now the church bells are ringing.

Act II · Sundays & saints

The Bells of the Island

Your first Sunday, and the whole religious architecture of Britain is on display: one church run by the monarch, one run by nobody in particular, a census that counts the faithful — and four patron saints queuing up across the calendar, two of them with a day off attached.

📊

Sunday morningThe census of souls

Who believes what? The survey the handbook quotes: 70% identify as Christian, 21% say they have no religion, 4% are Muslim, 2% Hindu, 1% Sikh, and fewer than 0.5% each are Jewish or Buddhist (with 2% for other religions). The UK is historically Christian but thoroughly multi-faith — your festival calendar in Act III will prove it.

🧠 Pin it: second chant of the chapter — 70 – 21 – 4 – 2 – 1, sliding downhill. Learn it beside 84–8–5–3 and you own both stat questions.

Seventy percent Christian — but Christian in which church? That question once cost a king his head, and it built the church on your corner…

⛪

Sunday, 11 amThe King's church

The Church of England is the established church — the official church of the state, Protestant, born in the 1530s Reformation (Chapter 3's royal divorce, remember). Abroad it's called the Anglican Church — and in Scotland and the USA, the Episcopal Church. Its head is the monarch; its spiritual leader is the Archbishop of Canterbury, chosen with the Prime Minister involved and appointed by the monarch. Several of its bishops sit in the House of Lords — church and state, still holding hands after five centuries.

⚠️ Test trap: the monarch is the head; Canterbury is only the spiritual leader. Swap them and the examiners collect their favourite wrong answer.

Cross the border north, though, and the same God runs on an entirely different org chart…

🏴󠁧󠁢󠁳󠁣󠁴󠁿

Sunday, in ScotlandThe Kirk and everyone else

Scotland's national church is Presbyterian — governed not by bishops but by ministers and elders, with a Moderator at its head who is appointed annually: no permanent boss at all. Wales and Northern Ireland have no established church whatsoever. And beyond the two national churches march the other Christian traditions: Baptists, Methodists, Presbyterians, Quakers and Roman Catholics.

🧠 Pin it: "Episcopal Church" is the Church of England's name in Scotland — the Church of Scotland itself is Presbyterian. Two churches share one border; keep their labels apart.

Every church here honours a national saint — and the four of them are queued across your new calendar…

🍀

The saints' queueDavid, Patrick, George… then a long wait for Andrew

Four nations, four patron saints, in strict calendar order: St David of Wales on 1 March, St Patrick of Northern Ireland on 17 March, St George of England on 23 April — and then nothing for seven months until St Andrew of Scotland on 30 November. Only the Celtic pair get a proper day off: Patrick's day is an official holiday in Northern Ireland and Andrew's in Scotland; England and Wales work straight through their saints' days.

🧠 Pin it: two saints in March, one in April, one alone in November — and only Patrick and Andrew bring a holiday. David and George clock in as usual.

Four saints are just the skeleton of the year. Now hang the rest of the calendar on it — every feast, fast and firework of your first twelve months…

Previously: you met the two national churches — the monarch's and the Moderator's — and queued up the four saints: 1 March, 17 March, 23 April, 30 November.

Act III · One year of festivals

The Wheel of the Year

Now live the whole calendar once, January to December. The test only ever asks two things about a festival: when is it, and what does it celebrate. Picture a single year and pin everything to it — fixed days on one hand, moving feasts on the other.

🎆

1 JanuaryMidnight — and Scotland does it bigger

Your year opens with fireworks. New Year, 1 January, is a public holiday, celebrated the night of 31 December. In Scotland that night has its own name — Hogmanay — and the party is so serious that 2 January is an extra holiday in Scotland only. One country, two days to recover.

The decorations come down, winter drags — and the shops fill with hearts…

💘

February → JuneSpring's little days

Valentine's Day, 14 February: cards and gifts, sometimes anonymous cards to secret admirers. April Fool's Day, 1 April: jokes on each other — but only until midday; an afternoon prank makes you the fool. Mothering Sunday floats — it's the Sunday three weeks before Easter — while Father's Day is anchored to the third Sunday in June. One parent moves with the moon, the other stays put.

🧠 Pin it: Mum floats (three weeks before Easter), Dad is fixed (third Sunday of June). And April foolery has a midday curfew.

And why does Mothering Sunday float? Because Easter itself floats — the great moving feast of the whole year…

🥞

March or AprilPancakes, ashes, and the moving feast

Easter lands in March or April and drags a whole season with it. The sequence: Shrove Tuesday — Pancake Day, eat up the rich food; then Ash Wednesday, when churchgoers take an ash cross on the forehead and Lent begins — the 40 days before Easter when people traditionally fast or give something up. It ends with Good Friday (marking Christ's death) and Easter Sunday (his rising). Good Friday and Easter Monday are public holidays, and Easter eggs symbolise new life.

🧠 Pin it: the chain is Pancakes → Ashes → 40 days → Friday death → Sunday rising. If you can say the chain, you can answer any Easter question they build.

Easter isn't the only feast that moves. Almost every faith festival in your new home floats with its own calendar — with exactly one exception…

🪔

Through the yearThe festivals of faith — and the one fixed date

Vaisakhi, the Sikh festival of the founding of the Khalsa, is celebrated with parades, dancing and singing on 14 April — the only faith festival with a fixed date. Everything else moves: Eid al-Fitr marks the end of Ramadan, thanking Allah for the strength to fast; Eid ul Adha remembers Ibrahim's willingness to sacrifice his son (in Britain, any animal sacrifice must happen in a slaughterhouse). Diwali — the Festival of Lights, October or November, 5 days — celebrates the victory of good over evil for Hindus AND Sikhs, most famously in Leicester. Hannukah, November or December, runs 8 days with a menorah candle lit each evening, remembering the Jewish struggle for religious freedom.

⚠️ Test trap: the two Eids are the exam's favourite swap — al-Fitr ends Ramadan, ul Adha is Ibrahim's sacrifice. And Diwali belongs to Hindus and Sikhs together, never Hindus alone.

The nights draw in, the clocks fall back — and the autumn throws its one-two-three punch…

🎇

31 Oct · 5 Nov · 11 NovAutumn's one-two-three

Twelve days, three fixtures. Halloween, 31 October — an ancient pagan festival: costumes, trick or treat, pumpkin lanterns. Bonfire Night, 5 November — fireworks remember 1605, when Guy Fawkes and his group plotted to blow up the Protestant king inside the Houses of Parliament (you met this plot in Chapter 3). And Remembrance Day, 11 November — honouring the war dead of WWI (ended 1918) and every war since: poppies on lapels, a two-minute silence at 11 am, wreaths at the Cenotaph in Whitehall.

🧠 Pin it: 31/10 → 5/11 → 11/11 — pagan night, gunpowder night, poppy morning. Three beats, twelve days, in that order every year.

And then the year closes the way years here always close — with lights in the windows and too much food…

🎄

24–26 DecemberDeep winter

On Christmas Eve (24 December) many Christians go to church. Christmas Day, 25 December — a public holiday celebrating the birth of Jesus Christ — brings roast turkey, Christmas pudding and mince pies, decorated trees, cards, and children hanging stockings for Father Christmas. Boxing Day, 26 December, is also a public holiday. Sprinkled through your year were the bank holidays too — no religious meaning at all: early May, late May or early June, and August; Northern Ireland adds the Battle of the Boyne anniversary in July.

🧠 Pin it: Christmas gives you two public holidays in a row — the 25th and Boxing Day the 26th. Bank holidays = May, May/June, August + NI's July Boyne day.

One full year lived. But wait — rewind to the summer. While you were busy with festivals, the whole country was watching something else entirely: sport…

Previously: you lived the wheel of the year — Hogmanay to Boxing Day, with pancakes, Vaisakhi's fixed 14 April, and autumn's 31/10 · 5/11 · 11/11 punch.

Act IV · The sporting year

The Games People Play

Sixteen champions, nine sports, three Olympic Games — and every single name in the handbook is welded to exactly one achievement. Learn the weld, ignore the biography. This act ends in the greatest sporting summer Britain ever had.

🏟️

Three GamesLondon calling — three times

The UK has hosted the Olympic Games three times: 1908, 1948 and 2012 — the last one based in Stratford, East London, where Britain finished third in the medal table. The Paralympics have British roots: they grew from the work of Dr Sir Ludwig Guttmann, a German refugee doctor at Stoke Mandeville hospital, who used sport to rehabilitate injured servicemen. The cathedrals of the sporting year: Wembley Stadium for football, the Millennium Stadium in Cardiff for Wales.

🧠 Pin it: 1908 – 1948 – 2012: forty years apart, then sixty-four. And the Paralympics = Guttmann + Stoke Mandeville — a refugee's gift to Britain.

The stadiums are built — now fill them, starting with the games the whole nation plays…

⚽

Saturday, 3 pmThe people's games

Football is the UK's most popular sport — its first professional clubs formed in the late 19th century, and each UK nation has its own leagues and national team. England's only international trophy is the 1966 World Cup, won at home with Bobby Moore lifting the cup as captain. Cricket originated in England; matches can last up to five days and still end in a draw — the most famous series being the Ashes against Australia (Sir Ian Botham captained England and set batting and bowling records). Rugby began in England in the early 19th century and split into two codes — union and league — with the Six Nations and Super League as their showcase competitions.

🧠 Pin it: five-day cricket can still end in a draw — the handbook finds this as funny as you do, and the test quotes it straight.

Those are the team games. But this island also invented an entire summer of individual sport — on grass, greens and gallops…

🎾

The old inventionsGrass, greens and gallops

Modern tennis evolved in England in the late 19th century — the world's first tennis club appeared at Leamington Spa in 1872, and Wimbledon remains the oldest tennis tournament in the world and the only Grand Slam played on grass. Golf traces to 15th-century Scotland; St Andrews is its home, and The Open Championship is the only golf "Major" held outside the United States. Horse racing goes all the way back to Roman times: Royal Ascot, the Grand National at Aintree, the Scottish Grand National at Ayr — with the National Horseracing Museum at Newmarket, Suffolk.

🧠 Pin it: three "onlys" — Wimbledon: only Slam on grass; The Open: only Major outside the USA; and 1872 Leamington Spa: the first tennis club anywhere.

Machines and oceans got their pioneers too — and one doctor ran his way into history at lunchtime…

⏱️

FirstsFour minutes, one world, no stopping

1954: Sir Roger Bannister becomes the first man to run a mile in under four minutes. On the water, Sir Francis Chichester sails single-handed around the world in 1966/67 — and two years later Sir Robin Knox-Johnston does it without stopping. On the track of a different kind, UK motor racing begins in 1902; Sir Jackie Stewart wins the Formula 1 championship three times, followed in the champions' club by Damon Hill, Lewis Hamilton and Jenson Button. Rowing has the annual Oxford–Cambridge boat race on the Thames; skiing has five centres in Scotland and Europe's longest dry ski slope near Edinburgh.

🧠 Pin it: Chichester sailed alone (1966/67); Knox-Johnston sailed alone without stopping. Same feat, one upgrade, two years apart.

Firsts turn into hauls — and Britain's medal collectors start stacking gold in numbers the test will absolutely quiz you on…

🥇

The collectorsKeepers of the gold

Number-code the champions and they can't trick you. Sir Steve Redgrave: rowing gold at five consecutive Olympic Games. Sir Chris Hoy: Scottish cyclist, six golds and one silver. Torvill & Dean: Olympic ice-dancing gold in 1984. Dame Kelly Holmes: two running golds in 2004 — the same year Dame Ellen MacArthur became the fastest person to sail around the world single-handed. In wheelchair athletics, two racers each won the London Marathon six times: Baroness Tanni Grey-Thompson (plus 16 Paralympic medals) and David Weir (plus six golds over two Games). And Ellie Simmonds took Paralympic swimming golds in 2008 and 2012.

⚠️ Test trap: Redgrave = 5 consecutive Games, Hoy = 6 golds — don't let the numbers migrate between names. And two different wheelchair racers won the marathon six times each.

All of it was rehearsal. In your first summer here, one golden fortnight put five names into the history books at once…

🌟

Summer 2012The golden summer

One year, five names — recite them like a relay team. Sir Bradley Wiggins: first Briton ever to win the Tour de France. Sir Mo Farah: Olympic gold in the 5,000 and 10,000 metres. Dame Jessica Ennis: gold in the heptathlon — seven events. Sir Andy Murray: the US Open, the first British man to win a Grand Slam singles title since 1936. Ellie Simmonds: gold again in the Paralympic pool. All of it in and around Stratford, East London, with Britain third in the medal table.

🧠 Pin it: Wiggins rode, Farah ran, Ennis leapt, Murray served, Simmonds swam — the 2012 five, one verb each.

The stadium lights go down, the evenings are yours again — and this island's other national sport begins: culture…

Previously: three London Games, a four-minute mile, the medal collectors — and the golden summer of 2012: Wiggins, Farah, Ennis, Murray, Simmonds.

Act V · Nights out

The Night Out

The densest name-list in the whole handbook — composers, painters, playwrights, novelists, poets. So we do it as one long night on the town: concert hall, gig, theatre, gallery, then home past the bookshop. The rule for every name: one person, one claim to fame. Learn the weld, skip the biography.

🎻

7 pm — the concert hallSeven composers, one chain

First stop, the orchestra — seven names in rough order of birth. Henry Purcell: organist at Westminster Abbey, a distinctly British sound. George Frederick Handel: German-born, a British citizen from 1727 — Water Music, Music for the Royal Fireworks, the oratorio Messiah. Sir Edward Elgar: Pomp and Circumstance — "Land of Hope and Glory". Gustav Holst: The Planets, with Jupiter reborn as the hymn "I Vow to Thee, My Country". Ralph Vaughan Williams: music steeped in English folk tunes. Sir William Walton: coronation marches. Benjamin Britten: the operas Peter Grimes and Billy Budd, plus A Young Person's Guide to the Orchestra — built on a Purcell theme — and the Aldeburgh festival in Suffolk. The season that binds them: the Proms, eight weeks of orchestral summer, run by the BBC since 1927, with the televised Last Night at the Royal Albert Hall.

⚠️ Test trap: Holst wrote The Planets; Elgar wrote Land of Hope and Glory — the single most-swapped pair in the chapter. And Britten's Young Person's Guide loops back to a Purcell theme.

The concert ends early. Good — because three streets away the amps are warming up…

🎸

9 pm — the gigFrom the Sixties to September's prize

Pop history in one breath: The Beatles and The Rolling Stones lead the British sound from the 1960s; punk kicks the door in during the late 1970s; boy and girl bands rule the 1990s. The big rooms: Wembley Stadium, The O2 in Greenwich, the SECC in Glasgow. The summer fields: Glastonbury, the Isle of Wight, the V Festival — and Wales's National Eisteddfod, a festival of music, dance and Welsh-language poetry. The silverware: the Mercury Music Prize each September for the best album from the UK and Ireland, and the Brit Awards for best British group and solo artist.

🧠 Pin it: decades in order — '60s Beatles/Stones → late-'70s punk → '90s boy & girl bands. Mercury = album, September, UK & Ireland.

Past the gig venue, the West End glows — Theatreland, where one murder mystery has refused to close since 1952…

🎭

11 pm — TheatrelandThe play that never closes

London's West End is "Theatreland", and its record-holder is Agatha Christie's The Mousetrap — running since 1952, the longest initial run in history. Before it came Gilbert and Sullivan's comic operas — HMS Pinafore, The Pirates of Penzance, The Mikado; after it, Andrew Lloyd Webber — Jesus Christ Superstar and Evita (both with Tim Rice), Cats, The Phantom of the Opera. At Christmas the family tradition is pantomime, where the Dame is a woman played by a man and the horse is two actors sharing a costume. Each summer the Edinburgh Festival takes over Scotland's capital — its Fringe is the experimental wing — and London's stage honours are the Laurence Olivier Awards, named for the great Shakespearean actor.

🧠 Pin it: Mousetrap = 1952 — the same decade-door as the Queen's accession. Lloyd Webber wrote with Tim Rice; Gilbert wrote with Sullivan. Keep the duos intact.

The morning after, hangover notwithstanding, you do the civilised thing — the gallery…

🖼️

Next day — the galleryTen painters and a shark-tank prize

Walk the rooms in order. Thomas Gainsborough: portraits in country settings. David Allan: Scottish portraitist — The Origin of Painting. Joseph Turner: modern-feeling landscapes that raised the whole genre. John Constable: landscapes of Dedham Vale on the Suffolk–Essex border. The Pre-Raphaelites — Holman Hunt, Rossetti, Millais — bright, detailed religious and literary scenes. Sir John Lavery: Northern Irish painter of the Royal Family. Henry Moore: huge bronze abstract sculptures. John Petts: Welsh engravings and stained glass. Lucian Freud: German-born portraitist. David Hockney: 1960s pop art. The houses that hold them: National Gallery, Tate Britain and Tate Modern in London, the National Museum in Cardiff, the National Gallery of Scotland in Edinburgh. And the annual controversy: the Turner Prize, founded 1984 for contemporary art, shortlisting four works at Tate Britain — winners include Damien Hirst and Richard Wright.

⚠️ Test trap: Britain's two famous German-born adopted sons are the composer Handel and the painter Lucian Freud. Constable = Dedham Vale; Turner = landscapes in general — don't cross the easels.

Out of the gallery, the city itself becomes the exhibition — every building on the walk home has a name attached…

🏛️

The walk homeStones, gardens and hemlines

The architecture timeline, street by street: medieval cathedrals still standing at Durham, Lincoln, Canterbury and Salisbury; the White Tower of the Tower of London, a Norman keep built for William the Conqueror. Then Inigo Jones with the classical Queen's House at Greenwich and Banqueting House in Whitehall; Sir Christopher Wren's new St Paul's Cathedral; the Scot Robert Adam, whose interiors (Dumfries House) influenced Bath's Royal Crescent. The 19th century goes gothic revival — the Houses of Parliament and St Pancras Station — and the 20th sends Sir Edwin Lutyens abroad to design New Delhi and home again to build the Cenotaph. In the gardens: Lancelot "Capability" Brown's natural landscapes, Gertrude Jekyll's colourful borders, and the Chelsea Flower Show. The moderns: Foster, Rogers, Hadid. And in the shop windows, British design: Thomas Chippendale (18th-century furniture), Clarice Cliff (Art Deco ceramics), Sir Terence Conran (interiors), and the fashion trio Mary Quant, Alexander McQueen, Vivienne Westwood.

🧠 Pin it: Lutyens is the double-badge man: New Delhi abroad, the Cenotaph at home — the same Cenotaph from Remembrance Day in Act III.

And the last shop with its lights on, as ever, is the bookshop…

📚

Midnight — the bookshopOne shelf of novelists

Every novelist is welded to a book — read the spines: Jane Austen — Pride and Prejudice, Sense and Sensibility. Charles Dickens — Oliver Twist, Great Expectations (he gave English Scrooge). Thomas Hardy — Far from the Madding Crowd. Robert Louis Stevenson — Treasure Island, Kidnapped, Jekyll & Hyde. Sir Arthur Conan Doyle — a Scottish doctor who invented Sherlock Holmes. Evelyn Waugh — satire, Brideshead Revisited. Graham Greene — Brighton Rock, Our Man in Havana. Sir Kingsley Amis — Lucky Jim. J.K. Rowling — Harry Potter. Plus Agatha Christie's detectives, Ian Fleming's James Bond, and Tolkien's Lord of the Rings — voted the nation's best-loved novel in 2003. The prize shelf: the Man Booker Prize for fiction, awarded since 1968 — winners include Ian McEwan, Hilary Mantel, Julian Barnes. Nobel laureates in literature: Golding, Heaney, Pinter.

🧠 Pin it: the prize triangle — Turner paints (1984), Mercury sings (September), Booker writes (1968). Three prizes, three art forms; never let the test cross the wires.

And on the top shelf, older voices — the poets who were here before any of them…

🪶

The top shelfThe poets' roll call

It starts with Beowulf, the Anglo-Saxon epic, and Chaucer's Canterbury Tales (while Sir Gawain and the Green Knight tells of King Arthur's court). Milton writes Paradise Lost from Protestant conviction; Shakespeare leaves plays and sonnets — 14 lines each. Wordsworth walks the Lakes; Sir Walter Scott sings the Scottish borders. The 19th century crowds in — Blake, Keats, Byron, Shelley, Tennyson, the Brownings — and then the trenches of WWI produce Wilfred Owen and Siegfried Sassoon. The moderns: de la Mare, Masefield, Betjeman, Ted Hughes. Many of them lie together in Poets' Corner, Westminster Abbey — where this act's very first name, Purcell, played the organ.

⚠️ Test trap: the WWI poets are Owen and Sassoon — Hughes and Betjeman come later. And a sonnet is 14 lines; the test likes that little number.
Field notes — the quoted lines to recognise

Browning: "Oh, to be in England / Now that April's there" · Byron: "She walks in beauty, like the night" · Wordsworth: "I wander'd lonely as a cloud" · Blake: "Tyger! Tyger! Burning bright" · Owen: "What passing-bells for these who die as cattle?"

The test shows the line and asks for the poet — recognise, don't recite.

Culture fills your evenings. But a British life is measured just as much by its days off — the food, the telly, the pub, and the green bits…

Previously: one long night out — seven composers, the Sixties sound, the Mousetrap, ten painters, one shelf of novelists — and the prize triangle: Turner paints, Mercury sings, Booker writes.

Act VI · Days off

The Great British Day Off

What the country eats, watches and drinks — with the age limits and fines the test adores — and then out the door: fifteen national parks, one very old Trust, and the nine landmarks the handbook names. This is the act where your year turns into a life.

🍽️

Sunday lunchThe table — one dish, one flower per nation

Each nation brings a plate. England: roast beef with Yorkshire pudding (oven-baked batter) — and, of course, fish and chips. Wales: Welsh cakes, of flour, dried fruits and spices. Scotland: haggis — a sheep's stomach stuffed with offal, suet, onions and oatmeal. Northern Ireland: the Ulster fry — the full works, with soda and potato bread. Each nation wears a flower too, pinned on for its saint's day: England the rose, Scotland the thistle, Wales the daffodil, Northern Ireland the shamrock — grown grandly at Kew, Sissinghurst and Hidcote (England), Crathes and Inveraray (Scotland), Bodnant Garden (Wales) and Mount Stewart (NI).

🧠 Pin it: rose–thistle–daffodil–shamrock, worn on the saint's days you learned in Act II. The calendar and the buttonhole are the same fact.

Lunch digested, curtains drawn — a hundred years of British screens are waiting…

🎬

Since 1896The pictures

Films were first shown publicly in the UK in 1896. Charlie Chaplin becomes silent film's tramp; Alfred Hitchcock directs here before Hollywood claims him (he died in 1980). In WWII, In Which We Serve lifts wartime morale. Ealing Studios claims to be the oldest continuously working film studio in the world; Nick Park wins four Oscars, three of them for Wallace and Gromit; and the two biggest UK franchises are Harry Potter and James Bond. Recent British Oscar-winners: Colin Firth, Sir Anthony Hopkins, Dame Judi Dench, Kate Winslet, Tilda Swinton. The homegrown honours are the BAFTAs — Britain's Oscars.

Field notes — the named films (year · title · director)

1935 · The 39 Steps · Hitchcock — 1945 · Brief Encounter · Lean — 1949 · The Third Man · Reed — 1954 · The Belles of St Trinian's · Launder — 1962 · Lawrence of Arabia · Lean — 1969 · Women in Love · Russell — 1973 · Don't Look Now · Roeg — 1981 · Chariots of Fire · Hudson — 1984 · The Killing Fields · Joffé — 1994 · Four Weddings and a Funeral · Newell — 2003 · Touching the Void · MacDonald.

David Lean appears twice — Brief Encounter and Lawrence of Arabia. The test usually shows a title and asks the year or director.

The film ends, the telly goes on — and with it the one fine the handbook prints in bold…

📺

1969 · £1,000The telly and the licence

British comedy has a family tree: medieval jesters → Shakespeare's comic characters → 18th-century political cartoons → the satirical magazines Punch (1840s) and Private Eye → and in 1969, Monty Python's Flying Circus, television's great leap forward. Watching any of it needs a TV licence: one covers every set in one home (separate tenancies need their own), and watching without one risks a fine of up to £1,000. Over-75s get theirs free; blind viewers get 50% off. The licence fee funds the BBC — the only wholly state-funded media organisation independent of government. The social networks the handbook names, charmingly: Facebook and Twitter.

🧠 Pin it: comedy's chain ends Punch → Private Eye → Python 1969. Licence: £1,000 max fine, free at 75+, half-price for blind viewers.

Enough sitting down. The evening calls for the great British institution on the corner…

🍺

Opening timeThe pub rulebook

Pubs usually open at 11 am (noon on Sundays). The age ladder: at 18 you can buy alcohol and enter betting shops and gambling clubs; at 16 you may drink wine or beer with a meal in a hotel or restaurant — with someone over 18 — and play the National Lottery (under-16s cannot). Outside, even the dog is regulated: in public it must wear a collar with the owner's name and address, and the owner must control it and clean up after it.

🧠 Pin it: everything self-service is 18; everything supervised is 16. That one sentence decides every age question in this act.

And when the city finally wears you out, the island keeps its best medicine outdoors…

🥾

The green bitsFifteen parks and three volunteers

Britain walks. A vast network of public footpaths crosses the country, and 15 national parks protect the wildest of it — in England, Wales and Scotland (the handbook doesn't count any in NI). Guarding the rest is the National Trust, founded in 1895 by just three volunteers — today it runs on more than 61,000 of them, preserving buildings, coastline and countryside.

⚠️ Test trap: 15 parks — England, Wales and Scotland only. And the Trust's numbers rhyme: 3 volunteers then, 61,000+ now.

So lace your boots — your year ends with the grand tour itself: the nine landmarks the handbook names, one weekend each…

🗼

The final tourNine wonders of the UK

London first: Big Ben — properly the great bell, over 150 years old, its tower renamed Elizabeth Tower for the 2012 Diamond Jubilee; the Tower of London, begun in 1066 for William the Conqueror, home of the Crown Jewels, toured by Yeoman Warders (Beefeaters); and the London Eye — 443 ft (135 m), built for the millennium. Then outward: the Eden Project's giant plant biomes in Cornwall (a charity); Edinburgh Castle, medieval, kept by Historic Scotland; Loch Lomond — the largest expanse of fresh water in mainland Britain, inside a 720 sq-mile park; the Giant's Causeway in Northern Ireland, rock columns formed 50 million years ago; Snowdonia in Wales — 838 sq miles, crowned by Snowdon, Wales's highest mountain; and England's Lake District — 885 sq miles, its largest park, with Windermere the best-known lake and Wastwater voted Britain's favourite view in 2007.

⚠️ Test trap: Loch Lomond = largest fresh water in mainland Britain; the Lake District = England's largest park (885 vs Snowdonia's 838). Two superlatives, two different countries — keep them apart.

And that's the year gone — twelve months, four nations, one very full diary. Time to close it the way all diaries close: with the summary page…

Epilogue

The whole year in one breath

You land among four capitals and a chant — 84–8–5–3 — with eight coins, four notes in your pocket and a Victorian population curve (8 → 20 → 40) behind the crowd. Sunday teaches you two churches — the monarch's (1530s) and the Moderator's — and queues four saints: 1 March, 17 March, 23 April, 30 November, holidays only for Patrick and Andrew. Then the year turns: Hogmanay, hearts on 14 February, pancakes before Lent's 40 days, Vaisakhi fixed on 14 April while every other faith festival floats, autumn's punch of 31/10 · 5/11 · 11/11, and two holidays wrapped in tinsel at Christmas. Summer belongs to sport — three London Games (1908 · 1948 · 2012), Bannister's four minutes (1954), Moore's World Cup (1966), Redgrave's five and Hoy's six, and the golden 2012 five: Wiggins, Farah, Ennis, Murray, Simmonds. Evenings belong to culture — seven composers under the Proms (BBC since 1927), the Mousetrap running since 1952, ten painters and a Turner Prize (1984), a Booker shelf (since 1968) and poets asleep in Westminster Abbey. And days off belong to the island itself: roast beef, haggis, a £1,000 licence fine, a pub ladder of 16 → 18, 15 national parks under a Trust founded by three volunteers (1895) — and nine wonders from Big Ben to Wastwater, Britain's favourite view.
