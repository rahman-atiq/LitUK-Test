The Honours Board

Nobody forgets that Chris Hoy won gold. They forget whether it was six or eleven — because eleven is his too, and six belongs to two other people as well. Nobody forgets The Planets either; they just hand it to Elgar. So this sheet indexes the whole prize-giving, work-making half of the handbook four ways: by prize, by work, by person, and by the number itself.

The Prizes

Eleven prizes in five families, and the exam nearly always asks the same thing first: which field is it for. Get the field right and most questions are already answered. Below the board, the winners' roll takes the second question — who won it, and when — and answers it name by name, including the many times the handbook gives no date at all.

Two

The Works

The prizes are the easy half. The harder half is the pile of books, poems, plays, symphonies, paintings, films and objects the handbook names — usually in a single clause, usually attached to a person you have to supply. So here every named work is listed with who made it and when, in one place, in one direction: work → maker → date.

Read this before the list The handbook dates the maker, not the work. Austen is 1775–1817; Pride and Prejudice has no date at all. Holst is 1874–1934; The Planets has none. That is why the date column below is mostly life dates — and why a question that offers you a publication year is offering you something the handbook never printed. Films are the exception: eleven of them, each with a year and a director, and those you should know exactly.

Unlike the tally below, the blank here is the name. The title stays on screen, because the exam never asks what a book is called — it asks who wrote it. Tap any blank to check just that one. Filtering and hiding work together, so you can drill one medium at a time.

The four pairs that get swapped
Learn these as pairs, not as facts Holst wrote The Planets. Elgar wrote Land of Hope and Glory. The most swapped pair in the chapter.

Turner painted modern landscapes; Constable painted Dedham Vale. Born a year apart, and the prize is named after Turner — for contemporary art, which he never made.

Handel and Lucian Freud were both German-born. Composer and painter, two centuries apart; Handel took citizenship in 1727.

Owen and Sassoon are the war poets. Ted Hughes and Betjeman are moderns, and neither went near a trench.

The Six Nations — countries: England, Ireland, Scotland, Wales, France, Italy

Two hooks, and you need both. Redgrave counts Games; Hoy counts golds — Redgrave/Games, Hoy/gold, the vowels match. And the London Marathon is six for both wheelchair racers, so that number can never tell Weir and Tanni apart. What does: Tanni's headline is 16 medals; Weir's is 6 golds over 2 Games.

Both cyclists total seven Olympic medals — Hoy's seven is 6 + 1, Wiggins' seven is just stated as seven. And hept- is Greek for seven: heptathlon, Ennis, 2012.

The rule that saves you: eleven is never an Olympic number. Hoy's eleven are world titles; Tanni's eleven are Paralympic golds. So if the question says "Olympic" and 11 is an option, 11 is the trap.

Five

The Year Line

Every dated prize, medal, record and work in one order — the two halves of this page on one spine. A haloed dot means two things happened that year: those are the ones to fuse into a single memory rather than keep apart. The eleven named films have their own dates in The Works and are left off here, or they would drown everything else.

  • 1066The Bayeux Tapestry · the White TowerOne year, two objects that still exist: the tapestry commemorating Hastings (in France), and the Norman keep built for William the Conqueror.
  • 1086The Domesday BookWilliam's survey of every town and village — people, land and animals. Twenty years after the tapestry.
  • 1727Handel becomes a British citizenGerman-born, and by now the composer of Water Music for George I. The other German-born Briton, Lucian Freud, arrives two centuries later.
  • 1840sPunch is first publishedThe satirical magazine; Private Eye follows much later.
  • 1853–56The Victoria Cross is introducedBy Queen Victoria, during the Crimean War, for acts of valour by soldiers.
  • 1872First tennis club — Leamington SpaWimbledon is the oldest tennis tournament in the world and the only Grand Slam on grass.
  • 1896Films first shown publicly in the UKNo maker, no title — the date is the entire fact, which is exactly why it gets asked.
  • 1902Motor-car racing starts in the UKA Formula 1 Grand Prix has been held here every year since.
  • 1907Kipling — Nobel Prize in LiteratureThe only literature Nobel the handbook dates. And it lands one year before the first London Olympics — 1907, then 1908.
  • 1908London Olympics — the firstOne of three.
  • 1928Fleming discovers penicillinWhile researching influenza. This is why 1928 feels like an Olympic year and isn't — it is the answer sheet's favourite fake Olympics.
  • 1927The BBC starts running the PromsAn eight-week summer season; the Last Night at the Royal Albert Hall is televised. 1927 is the BBC's Proms, not the BBC's radio — radio was 1922.
  • 1936The last British Grand Slam singles titleUntil Murray, 76 years later.
  • 1945Fleming — Nobel Prize in MedicineDiscovered between the wars, decorated at the end of the second: 1928 found, 1945 awarded.
  • 1948London Olympics — the secondForty years after the first.
  • 1953The structure of DNAAt British universities in London and Cambridge. Francis Crick, one of those awarded the Nobel, was British.
  • 1952The Mousetrap opensAgatha Christie's play — the longest initial run in history, and still running. Two years before Bannister, and still going when you sit the test.
  • 1954Bannister's four-minute mileFirst man in the world under four minutes.
  • 1966England win the World Cup · Chichester sets sailBobby Moore captains; the tournament was hosted in the UK. The same year, Chichester leaves to sail single-handed around the world.
  • 1968The Man Booker Prize beginsBooks in ’68, brushes in ’84 — Booker then Turner, sixteen years apart.
  • 1969Monty Python's Flying CircusA new, progressive comedy — and the same year as Ken Russell's Women in Love. Booker ’68, Python ’69: consecutive years, and the only place this page has two.
  • 1972Mary Peters — pentathlon goldHer only Olympic medal, and the handbook's only pentathlon.
  • 1981Chariots of Fire — Hugh HudsonOne of the handbook's named films. Some question banks add that it won an Oscar; the handbook itself only names the film, the year and the director.
  • 1984Turner Prize founded · Torvill & Dean take goldIce and art in the same year. Fuse them — they are never asked together, so the pairing costs nothing and buys you both.
  • 2000Mary Peters made a DameOf the British Empire — for sport, twenty-eight years after her gold.
  • 2004Holmes' double gold · MacArthur's record · Wiggins' first goldKelly Holmes takes two running golds; Ellen MacArthur becomes the fastest to sail solo around the world; Wiggins wins the first of three Olympic golds.
  • 2008Simmonds' first golds · Wiggins' secondEllie Simmonds, the youngest member of the British team, wins Paralympic swimming gold.
  • 2012London — the third Olympics, and the ParalympicsMain site Stratford, East London; Britain finished third in the medal table. Wiggins (Tour de France) · Farah (5,000 & 10,000) · Ennis (heptathlon) · Murray (US Open, plus Olympic gold and silver) · Simmonds (swimming).
  • 2003The Lord of the Rings voted best-loved novel · Touching the VoidTolkien wins the nation's vote, and Kevin MacDonald's film is the newest the handbook names. The one year that is a book and a film at once.

The three Olympic years said aloud: nineteen-oh-eight, nineteen-forty-eight, twenty-twelve. Two eights and a twelve — and 1928 is the impostor.

Six

The Traps

Where the marks actually go. Read this last, and read it again in the car park.

Trap 1 · the ones who never won
Agatha ChristieNo Nobel Prize in Literature. She wrote detective stories and The Mousetrap — the longest initial run in history, open since 1952. The Nobel three are Golding, Heaney and Pinter.
Richard BurtonNo Oscar. A celebrated British actor, and exactly the name they put beside Firth, Hopkins, Dench, Winslet and Swinton.
Emily WatsonNo Oscar. The other name used the same way.
Trap 2 · the numbers migrate between names
Don't let them move Redgrave = 5 — and it counts Games, not medals.
Hoy = 6 — and it counts golds. His 11 is world titles. His 7 is total medals.
Tanni = 16 medals, 11 gold, 5 Games, 6 marathons, 30 world records.
Weir = 6 golds over 2 Games, 6 marathons.
Wiggins = 7 medals, golds in ’04, ’08 and ’12.
Trap 3 · which prize, which field
One line each Turner → contemporary art · Booker → fiction · Mercury → best album · Brit → best British group / solo artist · Olivier → theatre · BAFTA → film · Nobel → literature (Golding, Heaney, Pinter, Kipling), medicine (Fleming), and the prize Crick shared for DNA — for which the handbook names no category at all.
Mercury versus Brit Mercury crosses the Irish Sea. The Brits don't. Mercury: one winner, one album, each September, from the UK and Ireland.
Brits: many categories, best British group and best British solo artist.
Trap 4 · the person is not what the sentence says
The classic true/false "Mary Peters was an author who was later made a Dame in recognition of her work promoting literacy." — False. She is an athlete; the Dame, in 2000, was for sport.

"The Brit Awards is an event where sports people are given awards." — False. Music.

"Mo Farah was the first person to sail around the world single-handed." — False. That is Chichester. Farah runs.
Trap 5 · read which Games they asked for
2012 was both London hosted the Olympics and the Paralympics in 2012, so "which international event was held in London in 2012" can correctly be either — read the options before you answer. And the Paralympic movement itself began in Britain: Dr Sir Ludwig Guttmann, a German refugee, at Stoke Mandeville hospital in Buckinghamshire.
Trap 6 · the two science Nobels are different questions
Fleming versus Crick Fleming — penicillin, found 1928, Nobel in Medicine, 1945. Born in Scotland.
Crick — the structure of DNA, 1953, at London and Cambridge. The handbook's emphasis is that he was British and was one of those awarded.
Swap them and you lose the mark: Fleming has the dates, Crick has the universities.
Trap 7 · the date belongs to the prize, not the winner
Four dates in the whole roll 1968 is when the Booker began — not when McEwan, Mantel or Barnes won.
1984 is when the Turner Prize was founded — not when Hirst or Wright won.
1953 is when DNA was solved — not when Crick's Nobel was awarded, which the handbook never says.
1928 is when penicillin was found; 1945 is Fleming's Nobel. He is the only winner here with both.

Winners the handbook actually dates: Kipling 1907, Fleming 1945, and the Victoria Cross to soldiers of the Crimean War. That is the complete list.
Trap 8 · a work with a year is nearly always a film
Where the real dates live Eleven films have years. Almost nothing else does. Books, poems, paintings and symphonies come with life dates for the maker and nothing for the work. So when a question dates a work, check the medium first: if it is not one of the eleven named films, or one of the handful of dated moments — 1952 Mousetrap · 1969 Monty Python · 2003 Lord of the Rings · 1896 first film showings · 1927 the Proms — then the year in front of you is decoration.
Where the question banks drift from the handbook. Three places, and it is worth knowing which is which. Ellie Simmonds — the handbook says 2008 and 2012; some banks add 2016 and say four golds. Kelly Holmes — the handbook says only "two gold medals for running"; some banks name the 800m and 1500m. Chariots of Fire — the handbook names the film, the year (1981) and the director (Hugh Hudson), but does not say it won an Oscar; some banks do. In the real exam, answer from the handbook.
