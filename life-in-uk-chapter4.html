Major cities

  • England: Birmingham, Liverpool, Leeds, Sheffield, Bristol, Manchester, Bradford, Newcastle upon Tyne, Plymouth, Southampton, Norwich
  • Wales: Swansea, Newport
  • Scotland: Glasgow, Dundee, Aberdeen
  • Northern Ireland: Belfast

Currency

  • The pound sterling (£) — 100 pence in a pound.
  • Coins: 1p, 2p, 5p, 10p, 20p, 50p, £1, £2
  • Notes: £5, £10, £20, £50

Languages & dialects

  • Welsh — spoken in Wales, taught in schools and universities
  • Gaelic — spoken in the Scottish Highlands and Islands
  • Irish Gaelic — spoken in Northern Ireland

Who lives where

  • England 84% · Scotland 8% · Wales 5% · Northern Ireland less than 3%
  • Post-war immigration: nearly 10% of the population has a parent or grandparent born outside the UK.

Women & equality

  • Discrimination is against the law.
  • Women make up about half the workforce.
  • Girls leave school, on average, with better qualifications than boys.
  • More women than men study at university.

Church of England

  • The official Church of the state (established church) — Protestant, created during the Reformation in the 1530s.
  • Known as the Anglican Church in other countries, and the Episcopal Church in Scotland and the USA.
  • The monarch is the head of the Church of England.
  • The spiritual leader is the Archbishop of Canterbury — chosen with the involvement of the Prime Minister and a Church-appointed committee (the monarch makes the appointment).
  • Several Church of England bishops sit in the House of Lords.

Church of Scotland & the rest

  • The national church of Scotland is Presbyterian.
  • Governed by ministers and elders; its head is the Moderator, appointed annually.
  • No established church in Wales or Northern Ireland.
  • Other Christian groups: Baptists, Methodists, Presbyterians, Quakers, Roman Catholics.

Memory hook “David, Patrick, George… then a long wait for Andrew” Calendar order: 1 Mar → 17 Mar → 23 Apr → 30 Nov. Two saints in March, one in April, one alone in November. Only the Celtic-fringe pair with holidays — Patrick (NI) and Andrew (Scotland) — get the day off.
Exam trap The monarch is the head of the Church of England; the Archbishop of Canterbury is only its spiritual leader — don't swap them. And “Episcopal Church” is the Church of England's name in Scotland — the Church of Scotland itself is Presbyterian.

The Christmas season

  • Christmas Day, 25 December — public holiday celebrating the birth of Jesus Christ. Roast turkey, Christmas pudding, mince pies; gifts, cards, decorated trees.
  • Children hang stockings for Father Christmas (Santa Claus).
  • Christmas Eve (24 Dec) — many Christians attend church.
  • Boxing Day (26 Dec) — the day after Christmas, also a public holiday.

Easter & Lent

  • Easter (March or April) marks Christ's death on Good Friday and rising on Easter Sunday.
  • Good Friday and Easter Monday are public holidays.
  • Lent — the 40 days before Easter; people traditionally fast or give something up.
  • Shrove Tuesday (Pancake Day) — pancakes eaten the day before Lent.
  • Ash Wednesday — Lent begins; churchgoers are marked with an ash cross on the forehead.
  • Easter eggs symbolise new life.

Faith festivals

  • Diwali (Oct/Nov, lasts 5 days) — “Festival of Lights”, celebrated by Hindus and Sikhs; victory of good over evil and the gaining of knowledge. Famous celebrations in Leicester.
  • Hannukah (Nov/Dec, 8 days) — Jewish struggle for religious freedom; a candle lit each day on the menorah.
  • Eid al-Fitr — marks the end of Ramadan; Muslims thank Allah for the strength to fast.
  • Eid ul Adha — remembers Ibrahim's willingness to sacrifice his son; in Britain animal sacrifice must happen in a slaughterhouse.
  • Vaisakhi (14 April, fixed) — Sikh festival of the founding of the Khalsa; parades, dancing, singing.

The secular calendar

  • New Year, 1 January — public holiday; celebrated the night of 31 December. In Scotland: Hogmanay, and 2 January is also a holiday.
  • Valentine's Day, 14 February — cards and gifts; sometimes anonymous cards to secret admirers.
  • April Fool's Day, 1 April — jokes only until midday.
  • Mothering Sunday — the Sunday three weeks before Easter.
  • Father's Day — the third Sunday in June.
  • Halloween, 31 October — ancient pagan roots; costumes, trick or treat, pumpkin lanterns.
  • Bonfire Night, 5 November — fireworks recall 1605, when Guy Fawkes and a group plotted to kill the Protestant king with a bomb in the Houses of Parliament.
  • Remembrance Day, 11 November — honours the war dead; began with WWI, which ended in 1918. Poppies, a two-minute silence at 11 am, wreaths at the Cenotaph in Whitehall.

Bank holidays

  • Public holidays with no religious meaning: early May, late May or early June, and August.
  • Northern Ireland also marks the anniversary of the Battle of the Boyne in July.
Memory hook Autumn's 1-2-3 punch: 31/10 · 5/11 · 11/11 Halloween, Bonfire Night, Remembrance Day — three dates twelve days apart. And keep Vaisakhi as the only faith festival with a fixed date (14 April); Easter, Diwali, Hannukah and both Eids all move.
Exam trap Diwali is celebrated by Hindus AND Sikhs — not Hindus alone. Eid al-Fitr ends Ramadan; Eid ul Adha is about Ibrahim's sacrifice — the test loves swapping them. Mothering Sunday floats with Easter; Father's Day is fixed to a weekday pattern (3rd Sunday of June).

Sport

Three Olympics · sixteen champions · nine sports

Big venues: Wembley Stadium and the Millennium Stadium, Cardiff. The UK has hosted the Olympics three times — 1908, 1948 and 2012 (main site: Stratford, East London; Britain finished third in the 2012 medal table). The Paralympics grew from the work of Dr Sir Ludwig Guttmann, a German refugee, at Stoke Mandeville hospital.

Cricket & football

  • Cricket originated in England; matches can last up to five days — and still end in a draw.
  • The most famous series: the Ashes, England vs Australia.
  • Football is the UK's most popular sport; first professional clubs formed in the late 19th century.
  • Each UK nation has its own leagues and national team.
  • England's only international trophy: the 1966 World Cup, hosted in the UK.

Rugby & horse racing

  • Rugby originated in England in the early 19th century; two codes — union and league.
  • Top competitions: the Six Nations Championship (union) and the Super League (league).
  • Horse racing goes back to Roman times; key events: Royal Ascot, the Grand National at Aintree, the Scottish Grand National at Ayr.
  • National Horseracing Museum — Newmarket, Suffolk.

Golf & tennis

  • Golf traces to 15th-century Scotland; St Andrews is the home of golf.
  • The Open Championship — the only golf “Major” held outside the United States.
  • Modern tennis evolved in England, late 19th century; the first tennis club: Leamington Spa, 1872.
  • Wimbledon — the oldest tennis tournament in the world and the only Grand Slam played on grass.

Water, motor & snow

  • Sir Francis Chichester sailed single-handed around the world in 1966/67; Sir Robin Knox-Johnston did it without stopping two years later.
  • Rowing: the annual Oxford–Cambridge boat race on the Thames.
  • Motor racing in the UK began in 1902; British F1 world champions include Damon Hill, Lewis Hamilton, Jenson Button.
  • Skiing: dry slopes across the UK, five ski centres in Scotland, and Europe's longest dry ski slope near Edinburgh.
Memory hook 2012 — the golden summer One year, five names: Wiggins (first British Tour de France), Farah (5k + 10k gold), Ennis (heptathlon gold), Murray (US Open), Simmonds (Paralympic pool). Add the venue — Stratford, East London — and Britain's third-place medal finish.
Exam trap Two different wheelchair racers won the London Marathon six times each: Tanni Grey-Thompson (also 16 Paralympic medals) and David Weir (six golds over two Games). Redgrave = 5 consecutive Games, Hoy = 6 golds — don't let the numbers migrate between names.

Classical composers — the chain

  • Henry Purcell 1659–95 — organist at Westminster Abbey; church music and operas; a distinctly British style.
  • George Frederick Handel 1695–1759 — German-born, became a British citizen in 1727; Water Music (George I), Music for the Royal Fireworks (George II), the oratorio Messiah.
  • Sir Edward Elgar 1857–1934 — born in Worcester; Pomp and Circumstance March No. 1 (“Land of Hope and Glory”), a Last Night of the Proms fixture.
  • Gustav Holst 1874–1934 — The Planets; adapted Jupiter into the hymn “I Vow to Thee, My Country”.
  • Ralph Vaughan Williams 1872–1958 — orchestral and choral music shaped by English folk music.
  • Sir William Walton 1902–83 — coronation marches for George VI and Elizabeth II; Façade, Belshazzar's Feast.
  • Benjamin Britten 1913–76 — operas Peter Grimes and Billy Budd; A Young Person's Guide to the Orchestra (on a Purcell theme); founded the Aldeburgh festival, Suffolk.

Pop, venues & prizes

  • The Proms — an eight-week summer season of orchestral music, run by the BBC since 1927; the Last Night (Royal Albert Hall) is televised.
  • The Beatles and The Rolling Stones led the British sound from the 1960s; punk hit in the late 1970s; boy and girl bands in the 1990s.
  • Venues: Wembley Stadium, The O2 (Greenwich, SE London), the SECC in Glasgow.
  • Festivals: Glastonbury, Isle of Wight, V Festival.
  • National Eisteddfod of Wales — annual festival of music, dance, art and Welsh-language performance and poetry.
  • Mercury Music Prize — each September, best album from the UK and Ireland. Brit Awards — best British group and solo artist.

Theatre

  • London's West End — “Theatreland”.
  • The Mousetrap (Agatha Christie) — running since 1952, the longest initial run in history.
  • Gilbert and Sullivan — comic operas: HMS Pinafore, The Pirates of Penzance, The Mikado.
  • Andrew Lloyd Webber — Jesus Christ Superstar and Evita (with Tim Rice), Cats, The Phantom of the Opera.
  • Pantomime — Christmas family shows from fairy tales; the Dame is a woman played by a man; the panto horse is two actors.
  • Edinburgh Festival each summer — its Fringe shows theatre and comedy, often experimental.
  • Laurence Olivier Awards — London stage awards named after the great Shakespearean actor.

Art & the Turner Prize

  • Medieval art was religious; many 16th–17th-century painters here were foreign — Holbein, Van Dyck.
  • Thomas Gainsborough 1727–88 — portraits, often in country/garden settings.
  • David Allan 1744–96 — Scottish portraitist; The Origin of Painting.
  • Joseph Turner 1775–1851 — modern-style landscapes; raised the whole genre's standing.
  • John Constable 1776–1837 — landscapes of Dedham Vale on the Suffolk–Essex border.
  • The Pre-Raphaelites — late 19th century; detailed, brightly coloured religious and literary scenes — Holman Hunt, Rossetti, Millais.
  • Sir John Lavery 1856–1941 — Northern Irish portraitist; painted the Royal Family.
  • Henry Moore 1898–1986 — large bronze abstract sculptures.
  • John Petts 1914–91 — Welsh; engravings and stained glass.
  • Lucian Freud 1922–2011 — German-born; portraits. David Hockney 1937– — 1960s pop art.
  • Galleries: National Gallery, Tate Britain, Tate Modern (London), National Museum, Cardiff, National Gallery of Scotland, Edinburgh.
  • Turner Prize — founded 1984 for contemporary art; four shortlisted works at Tate Britain; winners include Damien Hirst and Richard Wright.

Architecture & gardens

  • Medieval cathedrals still standing: Durham, Lincoln, Canterbury, Salisbury; the White Tower (Tower of London) is a Norman keep built for William the Conqueror.
  • Inigo Jones (17th c.) — Queen's House, Greenwich and Banqueting House, Whitehall, from classical models.
  • Sir Christopher Wren — the new St Paul's Cathedral.
  • Robert Adam (18th c., Scottish) — interiors and houses like Dumfries House; influenced Bath's Royal Crescent.
  • 19th century — the gothic revival: Houses of Parliament, St Pancras Station, Manchester and Sheffield town halls.
  • Sir Edwin Lutyens (20th c.) — designed New Delhi as India's seat of government and the Cenotaph in Whitehall.
  • Gardens: Lancelot “Capability” Brown — natural-looking landscapes; Gertrude Jekyll — colourful gardens, often with Lutyens; the Chelsea Flower Show.
  • Moderns: Sir Norman Foster, Lord Richard Rogers, Dame Zaha Hadid.

Fashion & design

  • Thomas Chippendale — 18th-century furniture.
  • Clarice Cliff — Art Deco ceramics.
  • Sir Terence Conran — 20th-century interior design.
  • Fashion: Mary Quant, Alexander McQueen, Vivienne Westwood.

Novelists — name → book

  • Jane Austen 1775–1817 — Pride and Prejudice, Sense and Sensibility; marriage and family.
  • Charles Dickens 1812–70 — Oliver Twist, Great Expectations; gave English Scrooge and Mr Micawber.
  • Thomas Hardy 1840–1928 — Far from the Madding Crowd, Jude the Obscure; rural settings.
  • Robert Louis Stevenson 1850–94 — Treasure Island, Kidnapped, Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde.
  • Sir Arthur Conan Doyle 1859–1930 — Scottish doctor who created Sherlock Holmes.
  • Evelyn Waugh 1903–66 — satire: Decline and Fall, Scoop, Brideshead Revisited.
  • Graham Greene 1904–91 — The Heart of the Matter, The Honorary Consul, Brighton Rock, Our Man in Havana.
  • Sir Kingsley Amis 1922–95 — 20+ novels; Lucky Jim.
  • J.K. Rowling 1965– — Harry Potter; now also writes for adults.
  • Agatha Christie — detective stories read worldwide; Ian Fleming — James Bond; Tolkien's Lord of the Rings — voted the nation's best-loved novel in 2003.
  • Nobel literature laureates: Sir William Golding, Seamus Heaney, Harold Pinter.
  • Man Booker Prize — since 1968, best fiction from the Commonwealth, Ireland or Zimbabwe; winners include Ian McEwan, Hilary Mantel, Julian Barnes.

Poets — the roll call

  • Beowulf — Anglo-Saxon epic, still translated today.
  • Chaucer — The Canterbury Tales; Sir Gawain and the Green Knight tells of King Arthur's court.
  • John Milton — Paradise Lost, born of Protestant ideas.
  • Shakespeare — plays, long poems, and sonnets (14 lines).
  • Nature poets: William Wordsworth; Sir Walter Scott — poems (and novels) inspired by Scotland and border ballads.
  • 19th century: Blake, Keats, Byron, Shelley, Tennyson, Robert and Elizabeth Browning.
  • WWI poets: Wilfred Owen and Siegfried Sassoon wrote from the trenches.
  • Moderns: Walter de la Mare, John Masefield, Sir John Betjeman, Ted Hughes.
  • Many rest in Poets' Corner, Westminster Abbey.
  • Quoted lines to recognise: Browning's “Oh, to be in England / Now that April's there”; Byron's “She walks in beauty, like the night”; Wordsworth's “I wander'd lonely as a cloud”; Blake's “Tyger! Tyger! Burning bright”; Owen's “What passing-bells for these who die as cattle?”
Memory hook Prize triangle: Turner paints · Mercury sings · Booker writes Turner Prize = contemporary art (1984, Tate Britain, Hirst & Wright). Mercury Prize = best album (September, UK & Ireland). Man Booker = fiction (since 1968, McEwan/Mantel/Barnes). Three prizes, three art forms — never let the test cross the wires.
Exam trap Two famous German-born Britons: composer Handel (citizen 1727) and painter Lucian Freud. Holst wrote The Planets, Elgar wrote Land of Hope and Glory — swapped constantly. And the WWI poets are Owen and Sassoon; Ted Hughes and Betjeman are later.

Gardens & national flowers

  • England: Kew Gardens, Sissinghurst, Hidcote — flower: the rose
  • Scotland: Crathes Castle, Inveraray Castle — flower: the thistle
  • Wales: Bodnant Garden — flower: the daffodil
  • Northern Ireland: Mount Stewart — flower: the shamrock
  • National flowers are worn on each country's saint's day.

Traditional food — one dish per nation

  • England: roast beef with potatoes, vegetables and Yorkshire pudding (oven-baked batter); also fish and chips.
  • Wales: Welsh cakes — flour, dried fruits, spices.
  • Scotland: haggis — a sheep's stomach stuffed with offal, suet, onions and oatmeal.
  • Northern Ireland: the Ulster fry — bacon, eggs, sausage, black and white pudding, tomatoes, mushrooms, soda bread, potato bread.

Film

  • Films first shown publicly in the UK in 1896.
  • Charlie Chaplin — famous for his tramp character in silent film.
  • Alfred Hitchcock directed here before moving to Hollywood; died 1980.
  • In Which We Serve boosted wartime morale in WWII.
  • Ealing Studios — a claim to being the oldest continuously working film studio in the world.
  • Nick Park — four Oscars, including three for Wallace and Gromit.
  • Harry Potter and James Bond — the two highest-grossing UK film franchises.
  • Recent Oscar-winning actors: Colin Firth, Sir Anthony Hopkins, Dame Judi Dench, Kate Winslet, Tilda Swinton.
  • The British Academy Film Awards (BAFTAs) — the British Oscars.

Comedy, TV & social media

  • The comic tradition: medieval jesters → Shakespeare's comic characters → 18th-century political cartoons → satirical magazines: Punch (1840s), then Private Eye.
  • Monty Python's Flying Circus (1969) — a new, progressive comedy; sitcoms and stand-up thrive today.
  • TV licence: one covers all equipment in one home (separate tenancies need their own); watching without one risks a fine of up to £1,000.
  • Over-75s: free licence; blind people: 50% discount.
  • The BBC — funded by the licence fee; the only wholly state-funded media organisation independent of government.
  • Social networking: Facebook and Twitter.

Pubs, betting & pets — the rulebook

  • 18 to buy alcohol in a pub or night club.
  • At 16, you may drink wine or beer with a meal in a hotel or restaurant (including pub eating areas) with someone over 18.
  • Pubs usually open from 11.00 am (noon on Sundays).
  • 18 to enter betting shops or gambling clubs; under-16s cannot play the National Lottery.
  • Dogs in public must wear a collar with the owner's name and address; the owner must control the dog and clean up after it.

Countryside & the National Trust

  • A large network of public footpaths; mountain biking, mountaineering, hill walking.
  • 15 national parks in England, Wales and Scotland.
  • The National Trust — founded 1895 by three volunteers; now over 61,000 volunteers; preserves buildings, coastline and countryside.
