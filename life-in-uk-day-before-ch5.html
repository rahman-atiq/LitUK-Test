Memory hook Scotland does everything a bit differently One rule of thumb saves eight facts: in Scotland it's JP Courts (not magistrates'), the Sheriff Court (not Crown/County), 15 jurors (not 12), three verdicts (“not proven”), a £5,000 small-claims limit (England & Wales allow £10,000), and Children's Hearings for the young.
The courts exam trap is in section 2 →

Judges interpret the law and keep trials fair — government cannot interfere; if the government acts illegally, it must change its policies or ask Parliament to change the law. Jury service is compulsory for those summoned, unless ineligible or excused for good reason. Solicitors are trained lawyers who advise, act and represent; fees usually follow time spent.

That is every court the chapter names. Scotland's High Court is a first-instance court for the worst crimes, not an appeal court: there is no appeal tier in Chapter 5 — no Supreme Court, no Court of Appeal — so there is none on the grid or the map. If a court isn't here, the chapter never says it.

The Traps

All six of the chapter's confusable pairs, each with the memory hook that defends it, in chapter order. If you read nothing else, read this.

The ConstitutionMonarchy · Parliament
Memory hook 1958 gives life · 1999 takes inheritance Lords reform in two beats: 1958 — life peers arrive (PM nominates, monarch appoints). 1999 — hereditary peers lose their automatic seats. And the monarch's three verbs: advise, warn, encourage — never “decide”.
Exam trap The constitution is unwritten because Britain never had a revolution — not because nobody wrote it down. First past the post elects MPs; proportional representation elects MEPs (and the devolved bodies). The Speaker is neutral and chosen by secret ballot of MPs — not appointed by the PM or the Queen.
The GovernmentPM · devolution · voting
Memory hook The devolution phone number: 60 – 129 – 108 West to east then across the water: Wales 60, Scotland 129, NI 108. Scotland got its parliament in 1999, NI's deal was 1998 (Good Friday), Wales levelled up in 2011 (laws in 20 areas). Westminster keeps D-F-I-T-S: defence, foreign affairs, immigration, taxation, social security.
Exam trap EU citizens vote in local and devolved elections but NOT general elections — Commonwealth and Irish citizens can vote in all. The Home Secretary handles immigration (not the Foreign Secretary); the Chancellor handles the economy. Only Scotland's parliament can raise taxes among the devolved bodies named here.
The UK & the WorldFive clubs, five numbers
Memory hook Descending staircase: 190+ → 54 → 47 → 27 → 15 → 5 UN members → Commonwealth → Council of Europe → EU → UN Security Council seats → its permanent members (UK included). Six numbers, biggest to smallest — recite it as one breath.
Exam trap The Council of Europe is NOT the EU — it has 47 members, makes no laws, and wrote the European Convention on Human Rights. The Commonwealth has no power over members (it can only suspend). Rome was signed in 1957 without the UK; Britain arrived 16 years later, in 1973.

Three

Who Does What

Chapter 5's cast is roles, not people — so this is a map of offices, and of who chooses whom. The test asks the direction as often as the name: PCCs are elected by the public and appoint Chief Constables, not the reverse. Every chain below runs the way the power runs.

Voters elect → MPs monarch invites the leader with most MPs → Prime Minister appoints → Cabinet

That is the whole spine of the chapter: the monarch appoints the government the people have elected — she does not choose it.

The constitution

A constitution is a set of principles by which a country is governed. Britain's is unwritten — not codified in a single document, because the country avoided revolution and its institutions developed gradually over centuries.

The monarchQueen since 1952

Head of state of the UK and many Commonwealth countries. A constitutional monarchy: she does not rule. She appoints the government the people have elected, may “advise, warn and encourage” the PM, opens Parliament each year with the Queen's Speech, and every Act of Parliament is made in her name. Receives foreign ambassadors, hosts heads of state, makes state visits — the focus for national identity and pride.

chosen byInheritance — Elizabeth II became queen on her father's death in 1952 (Diamond Jubilee, 60 years, in 2012); married to Prince Philip, Duke of Edinburgh; heir Prince Charles, the Prince of Wales.
House of Commons — MPsthe more important House

The more important chamber because its members are democratically elected. Each MP represents a parliamentary constituency; the PM and almost all cabinet members are MPs. MPs represent everyone in their constituency, help create new laws, scrutinise and question government, and debate the big issues.

chosen byThe voters of each constituency, first past the post, at a general election at least every five years. A by-election is held when an MP dies or resigns.
The Speakerneutral

Chairs debates and keeps order; is neutral despite being an MP; guarantees the opposition debate time; represents Parliament on ceremonial occasions.

chosen byOther MPs, in a secret ballot — not by the PM and not by the Queen.
House of Lords — peers1958 · 1999

Members are peers — not elected, and with no constituency. More independent than the Commons: proposes amendments, checks laws, holds government to account, brings specialist knowledge. The Commons can overrule the Lords, but rarely does.

chosen bySince 1958, life peers are nominated by the PM — also by other party leaders and an independent Appointments Commission — and appointed by the monarch for their lifetime. Before 1958: hereditary title-holders, senior judges and Church of England bishops. Since 1999 hereditary peers have lost the automatic right to attend and now elect a few representatives.
PM & party leaders nominate → The monarch appoints → Life peers
Elections & the electorate18+, since 1969

General elections at least every five years; the party with a majority of constituencies forms the government, and no majority may mean a coalition. European Parliament elections run every five years by proportional representation, and its members are MEPs. Polling stations (polling places in Scotland) open 7 am–10 pm; NI voters need photo ID; postal voting is available.

who votesUK-born and naturalised citizens plus resident Commonwealth and Irish citizens; resident EU citizens can vote in all elections EXCEPT general elections. Register through your council's electoral registration office — a form goes to every household each September/October, and Northern Ireland registers individually.
Standing for office18+

You must be 18 or over and a UK, Irish or Commonwealth citizen. Exceptions include armed forces, civil servants and people with certain convictions; members of the Lords cannot stand for the Commons.

Government
Prime MinisterNo. 10 · Chequers

Leader of the party in power. Appoints the cabinet and controls important public appointments; lives at 10 Downing Street with a country house, Chequers.

chosen byThe electorate, via their party — the monarch invites the leader who commands most MPs (or a coalition). Can be changed by party MPs or by resignation, usually after losing an election.
The cabinet~20 senior MPs

About 20 senior MPs made ministers in charge of departments; meets weekly; big decisions often need Parliament.

  • Chancellor of the Exchequer — the economy
  • Home Secretary — crime, policing, immigration
  • Foreign Secretary — relations with foreign countries
  • Other Secretaries of State — education, health, defence. Departments also have Ministers of State and Parliamentary Under-Secretaries.
chosen byThe Prime Minister.
The opposition2nd-largest party

The second-largest party. Its leader challenges the PM at Prime Minister's Questions every sitting week and appoints a shadow cabinet.

chosen byThe voters — whoever comes second. Its leader appoints the shadow cabinet, exactly as the PM appoints the cabinet.
The civil servicepolitically neutral

Deliver and develop policy, and are accountable to ministers. Values: integrity, honesty, objectivity, impartiality.

chosen byAppointment on merit, and they stay politically neutral — they serve whichever government the voters return.
Voters elect → MPs the PM appoints ministers from among them → Ministers accountable to them → Civil servants
Local authoritieselections in May

Elected councils funded by central government and local taxes. Some areas have both district and county councils; many appoint a ceremonial mayor. London has 33 local authorities plus the Greater London Authority and Mayor of London.

chosen byLocal voters, at council elections held in May.
Parties & pressure groupsoutside government

Main parties: Conservative, Labour, Liberal Democrats, plus parties for Scottish, Welsh and Northern Irish interests; independents stand on local issues. Pressure and lobby groups try to influence policy — the CBI (business), Greenpeace (environment), Liberty (human rights).

The media & governmentfree press

Parliamentary proceedings are broadcast on TV and published in Hansard. The UK has a free press — newspapers are free of government control, though owners and editors may campaign. Radio and TV coverage must be balanced: rival viewpoints get equal time.

Devolved administrations — since 1997

Westminster keeps defence, foreign affairs, immigration, taxation and social security. The devolved bodies run much of the rest, including education.

Voters elect by proportional representation → 60 AMs 129 MSPs 108 MLAs
Wales — the National Assembly60 AMs · Cardiff

60 Assembly Members (AMs), elected every four years by proportional representation; works in Welsh and English. Since 2011 it can pass laws in 20 areas without the UK Parliament's agreement.

Scotland — the Scottish Parliament129 MSPs · Holyrood

Established 1999 at Holyrood, Edinburgh: 129 MSPs elected by proportional representation. Legislates on everything not reserved — civil and criminal law, health, education, planning — and is the one devolved body that can raise taxes.

Northern Ireland — the Assembly108 MLAs · Stormont

An earlier NI Parliament (1922) was abolished in 1972; today's Assembly followed the Belfast (Good Friday) Agreement, 1998. 108 MLAs at Stormont, elected by proportional representation, governing by power-sharing. The UK government can suspend it — it has run continuously since 2007.

Policing

Three duties: protect life and property · keep the peace · prevent and detect crime. Forces are independent of government, and officers must obey the law themselves — no misuse of authority, false statements, rudeness or racial discrimination.

The public elects (first elections Nov 2012, E&W) → Police and Crime Commissioner appoints → Chief Constable leads → The force
Chief Constableslead the force

Each police force is led by a Chief Constable and is independent of government.

chosen byThe Police and Crime Commissioner — appointed, not elected.
Police and Crime Commissionersfirst elected Nov 2012

Set local priorities and budgets and appoint Chief Constables. First elected November 2012 in England & Wales.

chosen byThe public, at an election — the direction the test asks about.
PCSOscommunity support

Community support officers: patrol, work with the public and support officers at crime scenes.

Complaints — three bodies, one eachby jurisdiction

Complain at the station or to the Chief Constable — or go to the body for your jurisdiction:

  • England & Wales — the Independent Police Complaints Commission
  • Scotland — the Police Complaints Commissioner
  • Northern Ireland — the Police Ombudsman
Terrorismreport locally

The threats named: Al Qa'ida and like-minded groups, and Northern Ireland-related terrorism. Report extremist recruiters to your local police.
