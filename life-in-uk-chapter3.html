Stone Age

  • First people were hunter-gatherers; Britain was joined to the continent by a land bridge.
  • The Channel cut Britain off ~10,000 years ago.
  • First farmers arrived ~6,000 years ago, ancestors probably from south-east Europe.
  • Stonehenge (Wiltshire) — probably a gathering place for seasonal ceremonies.
  • Skara Brae (Orkney, north Scotland) — best-preserved prehistoric village in northern Europe.

Bronze Age

  • Began ~4,000 years ago when people learned to make bronze.
  • Lived in roundhouses; buried dead in tombs called round barrows.
  • Skilled metalworkers — tools, ornaments and weapons in bronze and gold.

Iron Age

  • Iron weapons and tools; still roundhouses, now in larger settlements — some fortified hill forts, e.g. Maiden Castle (Dorset).
  • People were farmers, craft workers or warriors; spoke a Celtic-family language (related languages survive in Wales, Scotland, Ireland).
  • Minted the first British coins, some inscribed with kings' names — the start of British history.

The Romans · 400 years

  • 55 BC — Julius Caesar invaded and failed; Britain stayed outside the Empire nearly 100 more years.
  • AD 43 — Emperor Claudius invaded and succeeded.
  • Boudicca, queen of the Iceni (eastern England), fought back — her statue stands on Westminster Bridge, London.
  • Scotland was never conquered; Hadrian's Wall built to keep out the Picts — forts include Housesteads and Vindolanda; now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
  • Romans stayed 400 years: roads, public buildings, a structure of law, new plants and animals.
  • First Christian communities appeared in the 3rd–4th centuries AD.

Anglo-Saxons

  • Roman army left in AD 410 and never returned.
  • Invading tribes: Jutes, Angles and Saxons — their languages are the basis of modern English.
  • By AD 600, Anglo-Saxon kingdoms established, mainly in what is now England.
  • Sutton Hoo (Suffolk): a king buried in a ship with treasure and armour under a mound.
  • Wales and Scotland stayed free of Anglo-Saxon rule.
  • Christian missionaries — St Patrick (patron saint of Ireland), St Columba (founded the monastery on Iona), St Augustine (led missionaries in the south; first Archbishop of Canterbury).

The Vikings

  • Came from Denmark and Norway; first raided in AD 789 for goods and slaves.
  • Later settled in the east of England and Scotland — the Danelaw; Viking place names survive: Grimsby, Scunthorpe.
  • King Alfred the Great united the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms and defeated the Vikings.
  • Cnut (Canute) — the first Danish king of England.
  • In the north, the Viking threat pushed people to unite under Kenneth MacAlpin — the name Scotland came into use.

The Norman Conquest · 1066

  • 1066 — William, Duke of Normandy defeated King Harold (killed in battle) at the Battle of Hastings; became William the Conqueror.
  • The battle is commemorated in the Bayeux Tapestry, which still exists in France.
  • The last successful foreign invasion of England — changed government, society and language (Norman French influenced English).
  • Normans conquered Wales at first but the Welsh gradually won territory back; they took land on the Scottish border but did not invade Scotland.
  • Domesday Book: William's survey of every town and village — people, land and animals. It still exists today.
Memory hook "Caesar failed, Claudius nailed." 55 BC = failed visit. AD 43 = real conquest. And the three saints: Patrick → Ireland, Columba → Scotland (Iona), Augustine → south of England (Canterbury) — P·C·A, north to south.
Exam trap The Iron Age coins mark the start of British history — not the Romans. And Hadrian's Wall kept out the Picts (ancestors of the Scots), not the Vikings.

1066 → 1485 · the medieval period

An age of almost constant war — but also the birth of Parliament, English common law, the English language, and Magna Carta.

War at home

  • Wales: 1284 — King Edward I's Statute of Rhuddlan annexed Wales to the English Crown; huge castles built (Conwy, Caernarvon); last Welsh rebellions defeated by the mid-15th century; English law and language introduced.
  • Scotland: 1314 — Robert the Bruce defeated the English at the Battle of Bannockburn. Scotland remained unconquered.
  • Ireland: by 1200 the English ruled the area around Dublin known as the Pale.

War abroad

  • Knights fought in the Crusades — European Christians fighting for control of the Holy Land.
  • Hundred Years War with France actually lasted 116 years.
  • 1415 — Battle of Agincourt: King Henry V's vastly outnumbered army beat the French.
  • The English left France in the 1450s.

Feudalism & the Black Death

  • Feudalism (Norman system): king gave land to lords in return for soldiers; most peasants were serfs — tied to the lord's land.
  • Northern Scotland & Ireland: land owned by clans instead.
  • 1348 — the Black Death killed one third of England's population (similar in Scotland and Wales); one of the worst disasters ever to strike Britain.
  • Aftermath: labour shortages, peasants demanded higher wages; a new middle class and the gentry emerged; people moved to towns; the Pale shrank.

Parliament & the law

  • Parliament grew from the king's council of advisers (nobles and church leaders), consulted especially to raise money.
  • Split into two Houses: Lords (nobility, great landowners, bishops) and Commons (elected knights and wealthy townspeople) — only a small part of the population could vote.
  • Scotland's Parliament had three Estates: lords, commons and clergy.
  • Judges became independent of government; England developed common law (precedent and tradition); Scottish law was codified (written down).

Magna Carta · 1215

  • 1215 — noblemen forced King John to agree to a charter of rights.
  • Core idea: even the king is subject to the law.
  • Limited the king's power to collect taxes and to make or change laws without involving the nobility.

One English language

  • After 1066 the nobles spoke Norman French, the peasants Anglo-Saxon — they merged into English.
  • French gave us 'demand', 'park', 'beauty'; Anglo-Saxon gave 'ask', 'apple', 'cow', 'summer'.
  • By 1400 English was the language of official documents, the royal court and Parliament.
  • Scotland: Gaelic continued and the Scots language developed — John Barbour wrote The Bruce about Bannockburn.

Culture, castles & trade

  • Geoffrey Chaucer — The Canterbury Tales; among the first books printed by William Caxton, the first person to print books in England.
  • Castles for defence — Windsor and Edinburgh are still in use today.
  • Cathedrals with stained glass — Lincoln Cathedral, York Minster.
  • England's big export: wool. Skilled immigrants arrived: weavers from France, engineers from Germany, glass makers from Italy, canal builders from Holland.

Wars of the Roses · 1455–1485

  • 1455 — civil war for the throne: House of Lancaster (red rose) vs House of York (white rose).
  • 1485 — Battle of Bosworth Field: King Richard III (York) killed; Henry Tudor (Lancaster) became King Henry VII.
  • Henry married Elizabeth of York, Richard's niece — uniting the families as the House of Tudor, whose symbol is a red rose with a white rose inside.
Memory hook 1215 · 1314 · 1415 — the staircase of dates. Magna Carta 12-15, Bannockburn 13-14, Agincourt 14-15. Rising pairs, one century apart. Then the roses: red beat white, and Henry VII put white inside red.
Exam trap Bannockburn 1314 = Robert the Bruce beats the English (Scotland stays free). Agincourt 1415 = Henry V beats the French. Different century, opposite result for England. Also: the Hundred Years War lasted 116 years.

Break with Rome

  • Henry VII won the Wars of the Roses, curbed the nobles and built up the treasury; Henry VIII continued centralising power.
  • The Pope refused Henry's divorce from Catherine of Aragon → Henry created the Church of England, where the king, not the Pope, appoints bishops.
  • The Reformation swept Europe: Protestants read the Bible in their own languages and rejected praying to saints and shrines.
  • In Ireland, attempts to impose Protestantism caused rebellion and brutal fighting.
  • Wales was formally united with England by the Act for the Government of Wales — Welsh MPs entered the Commons.

Edward VI & "Bloody Mary"

  • Edward VI, strongly Protestant — the Book of Common Prayer written in his reign; died aged 15 after ~6 years.
  • Mary I, a devout Catholic, persecuted Protestants — nicknamed "Bloody Mary"; died after a short reign.

Elizabeth I · the Armada

  • Daughter of Henry VIII and Anne Boleyn; Protestant; re-established the Church of England.
  • Skilfully balanced Catholic and extreme-Protestant views — avoided religious war in England; one of the most popular monarchs ever.
  • 1588 — England defeated the Spanish Armada, sent to conquer England and restore Catholicism.
  • Elizabethan age: patriotism, exploration, poetry and drama; Sir Francis Drake's ship the Golden Hind was among the first to circumnavigate the world; English settlers began colonising eastern America.

Mary, Queen of Scots

  • 1560 — the Scottish Parliament abolished the Pope's authority; Catholic services became illegal; the Protestant Church of Scotland was not ruled by the state.
  • Mary Stuart, Catholic queen of Scotland, became queen at one week old; raised largely in France.
  • Her husband was murdered — she was suspected; she fled to England, leaving her Protestant baby son James VI as king.
  • Cousin Elizabeth I imprisoned her for 20 years, then had her executed for plotting.

James VI & I · union of crowns

  • Elizabeth died 1603 childless — her cousin James VI of Scotland became James I of England, Wales and Ireland; Scotland stayed a separate country.
  • The King James Bible ("Authorised Version") — translated in his reign; not the first English Bible, still used today.
  • Ireland: Henry VIII had taken the title King of Ireland; under Elizabeth and James, rebellions led to plantations — Protestant settlers (many from south-west Scotland) given land taken from Catholic landholders in Ulster, with serious long-term consequences.

Civil War → Republic · 1642–1660

  • James I and Charles I believed in the Divine Right of Kings — that the king was appointed by God and could rule without Parliament.
  • Charles ruled 11 years without Parliament; imposing a revised Prayer Book on Scotland's Presbyterian Church sparked rebellion, forcing him to recall Parliament in 1640.
  • Many MPs were Puritans (strict, simple Protestant worship); Parliament refused him money; rebellion broke out in Ireland; Parliament demanded control of the army.
  • Charles marched into the Commons to arrest five MPs — they'd been warned. No monarch has set foot in the Commons since.
  • 1642 — Civil War: Cavaliers (king) vs Roundheads (Parliament).
  • King defeated at Marston Moor and Naseby; by 1646 Parliament had won; Charles I executed in 1649.
  • England became a republic — the Commonwealth. Oliver Cromwell crushed Ireland with such violence he remains controversial there today.
  • Scots crowned Charles II king; Cromwell beat his army at Dunbar and Worcester; Charles famously hid in an oak tree before escaping to Europe.
  • Cromwell ruled as Lord Protector until his death in 1658; son Richard couldn't hold it together.

Restoration · Charles II

  • May 1660 — Parliament invited Charles II back from the Netherlands; he knew he could not always do as he wished.
  • The Church of England again the established church; Catholics and Puritans kept out of power.
  • 1665 — the plague; 1666 — the Great Fire of London destroyed much of the city incl. St Paul's, rebuilt by Sir Christopher Wren; Samuel Pepys diarised it all.
  • 1679 — Habeas Corpus Act: "you must present the person in court" — no unlawful imprisonment; every prisoner gets a court hearing.
  • The Royal Society founded — oldest surviving scientific society in the world; early members: Edmund Halley, Isaac Newton.

Glorious Revolution · 1688

  • Charles II died 1685 with no legitimate children → Catholic brother James II (VII of Scotland): favoured Catholics, arrested CoE bishops, ignored Parliament.
  • When James's wife had a son, a Protestant succession looked lost.
  • 1688 — Protestants invited William of Orange (husband of James's daughter Mary) to invade — no resistance, no fighting in England → "Glorious"; it guaranteed the power of Parliament.
  • 1690 — William defeated James at the Battle of the Boyne in Ireland (still celebrated by some in Northern Ireland); James fled to France.
  • Scotland: rebellion for James beaten at Killiecrankie; the MacDonalds of Glencoe were killed for being late to swear the oath — breeding distrust. James's supporters were the Jacobites.

William Shakespeare

1564–1616

Born Stratford-upon-Avon; playwright and actor. Midsummer Night's Dream, Hamlet, Macbeth, Romeo & Juliet. First to portray ordinary Englishmen and women; invented many English words. Lines to know: "To be or not to be" (Hamlet), "Once more unto the breach" (Henry V), "All the world's a stage" (As You Like It), "A rose by any other name", "The darling buds of May". The Globe Theatre in London is a modern copy of his theatres.

Isaac Newton

1643–1727

Born in Lincolnshire; studied at Cambridge. Wrote Principia Mathematica — showed gravity applies to the whole universe; discovered that white light is made of the colours of the rainbow. Early Royal Society member.

Oliver Cromwell

Lord Protector 1653–1658

Parliamentary general; victories at Dunbar and Worcester made him leader of the republic. Brutal campaign in Ireland; still controversial there. Succeeded briefly by son Richard.

Exam trap Same man, two numbers: James VI of Scotland = James I of England; William III of England = William II of Scotland (William of Orange). And keep the pair straight: 1665 plague → 1666 fire — disease before flames.

Constitutional monarchy

  • 1689 — Bill of Rights: confirmed Parliament's rights; the monarch must be Protestant, can't raise taxes or administer justice without Parliament, and must ask Parliament to renew army funding every year.
  • A new Parliament at least every 3 years then (later 7, now 5).
  • Party politics began: Whigs and Tories (Conservatives are still called Tories).
  • 1695 — newspapers freed from government licence: a free press.
  • But only property-owning men voted; pocket boroughs (controlled by one family) and rotten boroughs (almost no voters) persisted.
  • Migration: first Jews since the Middle Ages settled in London (1656); Huguenots — educated, skilled French Protestant refugees — arrived 1680–1720.

Union & the first PM

  • Queen Anne had no surviving children → 1707 Act (Treaty) of Union created the Kingdom of Great Britain; Scotland kept its own legal system, education system and Presbyterian Church.
  • Anne died 1714 → Parliament chose George I, a German Protestant; his poor English made him lean on ministers.
  • Sir Robert Walpole — the first Prime Minister, 1721–1742.
  • 1745 — Bonnie Prince Charlie (Charles Edward Stuart, James II's grandson) rose with Highland clans; crushed at Culloden, 1746.
  • Clans lost power → Highland Clearances: crofts destroyed for sheep and cattle; many Scots emigrated to North America.

The Enlightenment

  • 18th-century flowering of ideas in politics, philosophy and science — many of the greatest thinkers were Scottish.
  • Adam Smith — economics; David Hume — human nature/philosophy; James Watt — steam power that fed the Industrial Revolution.
  • Enduring principle: everyone has the right to their own political and religious beliefs — the state should not dictate them.

Industrial Revolution

  • Britain — the first country to industrialise on a large scale, powered by machinery and steam; before it, most worked in agriculture and home "cottage industries".
  • Bessemer process → mass steel → shipbuilding and railways.
  • Canals linked factories to towns and ports; conditions were dangerous, hours long, children worked and were treated harshly.
  • Colonies grew: Captain James Cook mapped Australia; Britain gained Canada; the East India Company took control of large parts of India; settlements in southern Africa.
  • Imports: sugar & tobacco (N. America, West Indies); textiles, tea, spices (India, Indonesia).

The slave trade — and its end

  • Slavery was illegal within Britain, but the overseas trade was dominated by Britain and its American colonies; slaves came mainly from West Africa to sugar and tobacco plantations in America and the Caribbean.
  • The Quakers set up the first formal anti-slavery groups (late 1700s); William Wilberforce, evangelical Christian MP, led the change in law.
  • 1807 — trading slaves banned on British ships and from British ports.
  • 1833 — Emancipation Act abolished slavery throughout the Empire; the Royal Navy freed slaves from other nations' ships.
  • After 1833, 2 million Indian and Chinese workers replaced freed slaves on Caribbean plantations, South African mines, East African railways and in the Kenyan army.

America & Napoleon

  • 13 wealthy, largely self-governing American colonies; Britain tried to tax them — "no taxation without representation".
  • 1776 — the colonies declared independence; they defeated the British army; Britain recognised independence in 1783.
  • 1789 — French Revolution; then war with Napoleon.
  • 1805 — Battle of Trafalgar: Admiral Nelson destroyed the French–Spanish fleet and died in the battle. His ship HMS Victory is at Portsmouth; Nelson's Column stands in Trafalgar Square.
  • 1815 — Battle of Waterloo: the Duke of Wellington ("the Iron Duke", later PM) defeated Napoleon.

The Union Flag · 1801

  • 1800 — Act of Union with Ireland (effective 1801) created the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland; the Union Flag ("Union Jack") symbolised it.

The Victorian Age · 1837–1901

  • 1837 — Victoria became queen aged 18; reigned almost 64 years — the longest reign of any British monarch (as the handbook records it).
  • The Empire grew to cover all of India, Australia and large parts of Africa — the largest empire ever, over 400 million people.
  • 1853–1913 — ~13 million Britons emigrated; 1870–1914 — ~120,000 Russian and Polish Jews fled persecution to London's East End, Manchester and Leeds.
  • Free trade: Corn Laws repealed 1846; 1847 — women and children limited to 10 hours' factory work a day.
  • UK produced more than half the world's iron, coal and cotton cloth; London became a hub for finance, insurance and banking.
  • 1851 — the Great Exhibition in Hyde Park's Crystal Palace (steel and glass).
  • Railways: pioneered by George & Robert Stephenson, spread across the Empire.
  • Crimean War 1853–56 (with Turkey & France, against Russia): first war covered extensively by the media; Victoria introduced the Victoria Cross for valour.
  • Boer War 1899–1902 in South Africa against the Boers (Dutch-origin settlers) — long, costly, and it made people question the Empire.

Ireland · the famine

  • Two-thirds of the Irish population depended on farming — many on potatoes.
  • Mid-century the potato crop failed: the famine — 1 million died, 1.5 million emigrated (to the USA and England). By 1861, large Irish populations in Liverpool, London, Manchester, Glasgow.
  • Irish Nationalism grew: the Fenians wanted complete independence; Charles Stuart Parnell championed Home Rule (an Irish parliament within the UK).

The road to the vote

  • 1832 — Reform Act: more people could vote; pocket and rotten boroughs abolished; seats shifted to the towns and cities.
  • The Chartists petitioned for the working man's vote — seemingly unsuccessful at first.
  • 1867 — second Reform Act: more urban seats, lower property bar — but most men and all women still couldn't vote; parties began building organisations to win the new voters.
  • 1870 & 1882 — Acts gave married women the right to keep their own earnings and property (before that it belonged to the husband).
  • The suffragettes campaigned for women's votes — 1918: women over 30 could vote and stand for Parliament (partly recognising WWI work); 1928: voting age for women lowered to 21 — equal with men.

Robert Burns

1759–1796

"The Bard" — Scottish poet; wrote in Scots and English. Auld Lang Syne, sung at New Year (Hogmanay in Scotland).

Richard Arkwright

1732–1792

Barber-turned-industrialist; improved the carding machine, ran water- then steam-powered spinning mills — remembered for efficient, profitable factories.

Sake Dean Mahomet

1759–1851

Born in Bengal; served in the Bengal army; came to Britain 1782; eloped with Jane Daly (1786). Opened the Hindoostane Coffee House (1810, George Street, London) — Britain's first curry house — and introduced "shampooing" (Indian head massage).

Isambard Kingdom Brunel

1806–1859

From Portsmouth; engineer of tunnels, bridges, ships and the Great Western Railway — Paddington to the south-west, West Midlands and Wales. Many of his bridges are still in use.

Florence Nightingale

1820–1910

Born in Italy to English parents; nursed soldiers in the Crimean War in Turkey (1854); founded the Nightingale Training School at St Thomas' Hospital (1860) — the founder of modern nursing.

Emmeline Pankhurst

1858–1928

Born in Manchester. Women's Franchise League (1889); co-founded the WSPU (1903) — the first "suffragettes": civil disobedience, hunger strikes. Died in 1928, weeks before women got the vote at 21.

Rudyard Kipling

1865–1936

Born in India. Nobel Prize in Literature 1907. The Jungle Book, Just So Stories, and the poem "If" — often voted a UK favourite.

Memory hook Two unions, two "-oh-" dates: 1707 and 1801. 1707 joins Scotland → "Great Britain". 1801 joins Ireland → "United Kingdom". And Napoleon's decade: 1805 sea (Nelson · Trafalgar) → 1815 land (Wellington · Waterloo).
Exam trap 1807 = slave trade banned (British ships & ports). 1833 = slavery itself abolished (Emancipation Act, whole Empire). Also: 1918 = women over 30 vote; 1928 = women at 21, equal with men.

Edwardian optimism

  • Britain in 1901: a global superpower — admired navy, thriving industry, strong institutions.
  • Social reforms: help for the unemployed, old-age pensions, free school meals, workplace safety laws, town planning against slums, support for divorced/separated mothers.
  • MPs were paid for the first time — opening politics to ordinary people.

The First World War · 1914–1918

  • Trigger: assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria, 28 June 1914; deeper causes — nationalism, militarism, imperialism, Europe split into two camps.
  • Allies included Britain, France, Russia, Japan, Belgium, Serbia, Greece, Italy, Romania and later the USA — against the Central Powers: Germany, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire, later Bulgaria.
  • The whole Empire fought: over 1 million Indians served, ~40,000 killed; plus soldiers from the West Indies, Africa, Australia, New Zealand and Canada.
  • Battle of the Somme, July 1916: ~60,000 British casualties on the first day alone; over 2 million British casualties in the whole war.
  • Ended 11.00am, 11 November 1918 — victory for Britain and the Allies.

Partition of Ireland

  • 1913 — Home Rule promised; the Protestant north threatened resistance; WWI postponed it.
  • 1916 — the Easter Rising in Dublin; leaders executed; guerrilla war followed.
  • 1921 — peace treaty; 1922 — Ireland split: 6 mainly-Protestant northern counties stayed in the UK as Northern Ireland; the rest became the Irish Free State — a republic in 1949.
  • Decades of conflict over a united Ireland became known as "the Troubles".

Between the wars

  • 1920s: better housing and living conditions; 1929 — the Great Depression: mass unemployment, worst in heavy industry (shipbuilding).
  • New industries thrived: cars and aviation — car ownership doubled 1m → 2m between 1930–39.
  • BBC radio began 1922; the BBC started the world's first regular television service in 1936.
  • Cultural bloom: writers Graham Greene and Evelyn Waugh; economist John Maynard Keynes.

The Second World War · 1939–1945

  • Hitler took power 1933, rearmed, occupied Austria and invaded Czechoslovakia; when he invaded Poland in 1939, Britain and France declared war.
  • Axis: Germany, Italy, Japan. Allies: UK, France, Poland, Australia, New Zealand, Canada, South Africa.
  • 1940 — Germany swept through France; Churchill became PM; Dunkirk: 300,000+ men rescued by the navy and small civilian boats — "the Dunkirk spirit".
  • Battle of Britain, summer 1940: the RAF won the air war with the Spitfire and Hurricane (both British-designed). Then the Blitz — night bombing of London (East End) and Coventry (almost destroyed) — "the Blitz spirit".
  • From June 1940 to June 1941 Britain and its Empire stood almost alone against Nazi Germany.
  • Japan defeated Britain at Singapore, occupied Burma, threatened India; the US entered the war after Pearl Harbor, December 1941; Hitler's invasion of the Soviet Union (1941) became his pivotal failure.
  • 6 June 1944 — D-Day: Allied landings in Normandy; Germany defeated May 1945.
  • August 1945 — atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki ended the war with Japan. British scientists led by Ernest Rutherford (Manchester, then Cambridge) had first split the atom, and worked on the Manhattan Project.

Winston Churchill

1874–1965

Soldier and journalist before becoming a Conservative MP in 1900; PM May 1940; refused to surrender; lost the 1945 election, PM again 1951, MP until 1964. State funeral, 1965; voted greatest Briton of all time in 2002. Know the three speeches: "blood, toil, tears and sweat" (first speech as PM) · "we shall fight on the beaches" (after Dunkirk) · "never… was so much owed by so many to so few" (Battle of Britain).

Alexander Fleming

1881–1955

Born in Scotland; doctor in London. Discovered penicillin in 1928 while researching influenza; developed into a usable drug by Florey and Chain; mass-produced in the 1940s. Nobel Prize in Medicine, 1945.

Memory hook WWI ends on elevens: 11am, 11th day, 11th month, 1918. And 1940 is Churchill's year of three: becomes PM → Dunkirk → Battle of Britain, in that order.
Exam trap TV dates: 1922 = BBC radio, 1932 = Baird's first TV broadcast (London–Glasgow), 1936 = BBC's first regular TV service. Three different milestones — the test loves swapping them.

The welfare state · 1945–1951

  • 1945 — Labour under Clement Attlee elected, promising the welfare state of the Beveridge Report.
  • 1948 — Aneurin (Nye) Bevan, Minister for Health, founded the NHS: minimum standard of health care for all, free at the point of use.
  • Benefits system built for security "from the cradle to the grave".
  • Nationalised: railways, coal mines, gas, water, electricity.
  • 1947 — independence for nine countries including India, Pakistan and Ceylon (Sri Lanka); other colonies followed over 20 years — a mostly orderly transition to the Commonwealth.
  • UK developed its own atomic bomb and joined NATO; Conservatives governed 1951–64; Harold Macmillan gave the "wind of change" speech on decolonisation.

Post-war migration

  • Labour shortages → 1948: workers invited from the West Indies (recruitment centres for bus drivers).
  • Textile and engineering firms sent agents to India and Pakistan; ~25 years of migration from the West Indies, India, Pakistan and later Bangladesh.
  • Late 1960s — immigration laws tightened (ties to Britain by birth or ancestry required).
  • Early 1970s — Britain admitted 28,000 people of Indian origin forced out of Uganda.

The Swinging Sixties

  • Growth in British fashion, cinema and pop — The Beatles and The Rolling Stones.
  • Laws liberalised: divorce and abortion (England, Wales, Scotland).
  • Women's rights: employers had commonly made women leave work when they married; Parliament legislated for equal pay and against sex discrimination.
  • Britain and France developed Concorde, the supersonic airliner; high-rise concrete-and-steel architecture appeared.

The troubled 1970s

  • Boom ended: prices rose, the pound wobbled, imports exceeded exports (balance-of-payments problems), strikes spread — unions were seen as too powerful.
  • Northern Ireland: 1972 — Stormont Parliament suspended, direct rule from London; ~3,000 people died in the violence after 1969.
  • Europe: the EEC was formed in 1957 (West Germany, France, Belgium, Italy, Luxembourg, Netherlands); the UK joined in 1973; the UK never adopted the Euro.

Thatcher years · 1979–1990

  • 1979 — Margaret Thatcher became the UK's first woman Prime Minister; the longest-serving PM of the 20th century (to 1990).
  • Privatised nationalised industries; legal curbs on trade union power; deregulation grew the City of London's financial services; traditional industries (shipbuilding, coal) declined.
  • 1982 — Argentina invaded the Falkland Islands; a naval taskforce recovered them.
  • Worked closely with US President Reagan; early to engage the changing Soviet leadership as the Cold War ended.
  • Succeeded by John Major, who helped begin the Northern Ireland peace process.

Blair, devolution & after

  • 1997 — Labour under Tony Blair: created the Scottish Parliament (substantial law-making powers) and the Welsh Assembly (fewer powers).
  • 1998 — Good Friday Agreement; Northern Ireland Assembly elected 1999, suspended 2002, reinstated 2007; most paramilitary groups decommissioned their arms.
  • 2007 — Gordon Brown became PM.
  • Conflicts: coalition to liberate Kuwait (1990); former Yugoslavia; Afghanistan and Iraq — British combat troops left Iraq in 2009; UK part of the UN-mandated ISAF force of 50 nations in Afghanistan.
  • May 2010 — first General Election with no overall majority since February 1974 → Conservative–Liberal Democrat coalition; David Cameron PM.

Clement Attlee

1883–1967

London-born barrister; Churchill's Deputy PM in the wartime coalition; PM 1945–51; led Labour for 20 years. Nationalised industry, created the NHS and the welfare state.

William Beveridge

1879–1963

Economist and social reformer; briefly a Liberal MP. The 1942 Beveridge Report named the five "Giant Evils": Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor, Idleness — the blueprint of the welfare state.

R. A. Butler

1902–1982

Conservative MP from 1923. The Education Act 1944 ("Butler Act") — free secondary education in England and Wales; the primary/secondary split survives today.

Dylan Thomas

1914–1953

Welsh poet. Under Milk Wood (radio play) and Do Not Go Gentle into That Good Night. Died at 39 in New York; memorials in Swansea.

Mary Peters

b. 1939

Born Manchester, moved to Northern Ireland. Olympic gold, pentathlon, 1972; later British team manager and promoter of NI sport; made a Dame in 2000.

Margaret Thatcher

1925–2013

Grocer's daughter from Grantham; chemist, then lawyer; MP 1959; Education Secretary 1970; party leader 1975; first woman PM, 1979–90.

Roald Dahl

1916–1990

Born in Wales to Norwegian parents; flew in the RAF in WWII. Charlie and the Chocolate Factory, George's Marvellous Medicine; many books filmed.

Memory hook The 1940s trio: 1942 · 1944 · 1948. Beveridge reports (1942) → Butler educates (1944) → Bevan heals (1948). Three Bs, in alphabetical-ish order, each two years-ish apart.
Exam trap Bevan ≠ Beveridge ≠ Butler. Beveridge wrote the report, Butler passed the Education Act, Bevan built the NHS. And the EEC: founded 1957 without the UK — Britain joined in 1973.
