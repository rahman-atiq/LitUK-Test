The Deal

The shortest chapter in the handbook — and the densest. It is, quite literally, a contract: Britain states its five principles, names the five duties you'll owe, and offers five freedoms in return. Then comes the small print — how you qualify — and finally the exam room itself, because this test loves asking questions about itself. Read it as a deal being struck, clause by clause, and the whole chapter fits in one handshake.

Act I · The terms

Three Lists of Five

The heart of the chapter — and of the whole handbook. Five principles the house runs on, five duties you take on, five freedoms you get back. Fifteen facts, one act. Recite the three lists and Chapter 1 is done.

🤝

The offerAn offer rooted in history

British society is founded on fundamental values and principles which all those living in the UK — not just citizens — should respect and support. Where do they come from? The handbook's list: history, traditions, law, customs and expectations. And one line stands in bold at the door: there is no place in British society for extremism or intolerance. That's the deal on the table. Now read the terms.

🧠 Pin it: the values apply to everyone living in the UK — the handbook says "living", not "holding a passport". And the count of places for extremism: zero.

Every contract opens with the governing principles — Britain's has exactly five…

🏛️

Clause 1The five fundamental principles

In order: 1 · Democracy. 2 · The rule of law. 3 · Individual liberty. 4 · Tolerance of those with different faiths and beliefs. 5 · Participation in community life. If you've read the other story chapters, you'll recognise all five as the endings of long plots — democracy from Chapter 3's Reform Acts, the rule of law from Magna Carta, tolerance from centuries of religious wars. Chapter 1 is where the whole saga gets distilled to five lines.

⚠️ Test trap: the one candidates forget is participation in community life — it's a full fundamental principle, not a nice-to-have. Chant D-R-I-T-P: Democracy, Rule of law, Individual liberty, Tolerance, Participation.

Principles bind the house — but a contract needs your signature too. Here's what you agree to owe…

📋

Clause 2The five responsibilities — what you owe

Your side of the deal: respect and obey the law; respect the rights of others — including their right to their own opinions; treat others with fairness; look after yourself and your family; and look after the area in which you live, and the environment. Notice the shape: two duties to the law and to others, one to fairness, one to your household, one to your street. From courtroom to compost bin in five lines.

🧠 Pin it: the fifth duty has two halves — your area and the environment. The test sometimes quotes just one half and asks you to complete it.

And no British contract would be complete without consideration — what the UK offers in return…

🕊️

Clause 3The five freedoms — what you get

In return, the UK offers: freedom of belief and religion; freedom of speech; freedom from unfair discrimination; a right to a fair trial; and a right to join in the election of a government. Three freedoms of and from, plus two rights — and yes, the handbook counts all five as the freedoms the UK offers you.

⚠️ Test trap: the fair trial and the right to join in electing a government both belong on the freedoms list. The test likes to disguise them as "merely legal details" — they're not; they're the offer.

Terms stated, offer made — all that's missing is the signature. Britain wrote one out for you in advance…

✍️

The signatureThe citizenship pledge

At the citizenship ceremony, new citizens recite the pledge: "I will give my loyalty to the United Kingdom and respect its rights and freedoms. I will uphold its democratic values. I will observe its laws faithfully and fulfil my duties and obligations as a British citizen." Read it slowly and you'll see the trick: rights and freedoms… democratic values… laws… duties — the pledge is the three lists of five, compressed into three sentences. Learn them as one unit and each half reminds you of the other.

🧠 Pin it: pledge ↔ lists, same content, two formats. If you blank on a list in the exam, silently recite the pledge and unpack it.

Deal agreed. But before Britain lets you sign for real, there's a qualifying question: can you actually live here in English? Enter the paperwork…

Previously: the terms of the deal — five principles (D-R-I-T-P), five duties, five freedoms — sealed with the pledge that contains all fifteen.

Act II · The paperwork

The Small Print of 2013

One requirement — English plus knowledge of life in the UK — two routes to prove it, and a year, 2013, that changed the rules twice. January opened a choice; October closed a loophole.

🛂

January 2013Two roads to residence

To apply for permanent residence or citizenship you must speak and read English and have a good understanding of life in the UK. Since January 2013 there are two ways to prove it. Route A — the Life in the UK test: its questions are written to require English at ESOL Entry Level 3, so passing it means no separate English test. It's mandatory for Tier 1 and Tier 2 work-visa holders on the points-based system — they get no choice. Route B — an ESOL course with citizenship: for applicants whose English is below Entry Level 3; the course improves your English and teaches life in the UK — and still ends with a test.

⚠️ Test trap: the ESOL route is not a shortcut past knowledge — it covers English AND life in the UK, and finishes with an assessment. And Tier 1 and Tier 2 must take the LitUK test itself, full stop.

Nine months later, the government looked at the two roads and decided one crossing wasn't enough…

🔩

October 2013The rules tighten — test AND B1

From October 2013, settlement requires both: pass the Life in the UK test, and provide evidence of speaking and listening in English at level B1 of the Common European Framework of Reference (CEFR). Sounds like a new hurdle — but here's the calming fact: B1 is the European name for the same rung as ESOL Entry Level 3. Two ladders, one height.

🧠 Pin it: January 2013 gave you a choice of routes; October 2013 said "test AND B1 — both". And B1 = Entry Level 3, same rung, two names.

Requirements met, there's nothing left between you and the application — except the forms, the fee, and one very important website…

📮

The applicationForms, fees, and the website to check

After passing, you send in application forms and supporting evidence — exactly which depends on your circumstances. A fee applies, and it varies by application type. All forms and the list of fees live on the UK Border Agency website — www.ukba.homeoffice.gov.uk — and because requirements can change, the handbook's advice is firm: always check the UK Border Agency website before applying.

🧠 Pin it: the handbook names the UK Border Agency as the authority — answer with the handbook's wording, even though the real world has since reshuffled the agencies.

Which leaves just one door between you and the deal: a quiet room, a screen, and twenty-four questions…

Previously: two routes since January 2013, both since October 2013 — LitUK test plus B1 — and B1 = Entry Level 3, the same rung with two names.

Act III · The exam room

The Test That Tests Itself

The Life in the UK test asks about its own format surprisingly often. Memorise the logistics like any other fact — one string of four numbers carries the whole act: 24 · 60 · 3 · 2.

📝

The formatTwenty-four questions — from the whole book

The test consists of 24 questions about important aspects of life in the UK. And here's the clause people miss: questions are based on ALL parts of the handbook — including the introduction. Yes, the bit before Chapter 1 is examinable. The handbook has no skippable pages, which is precisely why this story series covers even its smallest chapters.

⚠️ Test trap: questions come from the whole handbook including the introduction — any answer limiting them to "the testable chapters" is bait.

Twenty-four questions — but in which language, and behind which door?

🏢

The venueSixty doors, three tongues

The test is usually taken in English, but special arrangements can be made for Welsh or Scottish Gaelic — three possible languages, and you met all three in Chapter 4's language scene. It can only be taken at a registered and approved Life in the UK test centre — there are about 60 around the UK — and the UK Border Agency only accepts certificates from registered centres. Never take it anywhere else. Residents of the Isle of Man and the Channel Islands have special arrangements — fitting, since Chapter 2 taught you they're not part of the UK at all.

🧠 Pin it: languages = English, Welsh, Scottish Gaelic — the same trio as the road signs. Centres ≈ 60, registered only.

Door chosen, language chosen — the last hurdles are a booking screen and two documents in your pocket…

🪪

The dayBook online, bring two things

Booking is online only, at www.lifeintheuktest.gov.uk — read the instructions and enter your details correctly. On the day, bring identification and proof of your address. The handbook is blunt about the alternative: without them, you will not be allowed to take the test. That's the whole ritual: one string of numbers — 24 · 60 · 3 · 2 — 24 questions, ~60 centres, 3 languages, 2 documents.

⚠️ Test trap: booking is online only — a question offering phone or walk-in booking is lying to you.

And that's the deal from handshake to exam desk. Close the contract the way lawyers do — with the schedule of numbers…

Epilogue

The whole deal in one breath

Britain puts a contract on the table, rooted in history, traditions, law, customs and expectations, with zero room for extremism: five principles — D-R-I-T-P — matched by five duties you owe and five freedoms you're offered, all fifteen compressed into the citizenship pledge. To sign it you need English plus knowledge of life here: from January 2013, two routes — the LitUK test (written at Entry Level 3; compulsory for Tier 1 and 2) or the ESOL-with-citizenship course — and from October 2013, settlement means test AND B1, two names for one rung. Then the forms and fees at the UK Border Agency site, and finally the exam itself: 24 questions from the whole handbook including the introduction, at one of ~60 registered centres, in 3 possible languages, with 2 documents in your pocket — booked online only.
