The British Constitution

Unwritten · monarchy · Commons & Lords · elections

A constitution is a set of principles by which a country is governed. Britain's is unwritten — not codified in a single document — because the country avoided revolution and its institutions developed gradually over centuries. Its parts: the monarchy, Parliament (Commons and Lords), the Prime Minister, the cabinet, the judiciary, the police, the civil service, local government — plus the devolved governments.

The monarchy

  • Queen Elizabeth II — head of state of the UK and many Commonwealth countries; queen since her father's death in 1952; Diamond Jubilee (60 years) in 2012.
  • Married to Prince Philip, Duke of Edinburgh; heir: Prince Charles, the Prince of Wales.
  • A constitutional monarchy: the monarch does not rule — she appoints the government the people have elected, inviting the leader who commands most MPs (or a coalition) to be PM.
  • Meets the PM regularly and may “advise, warn and encourage” — but policy belongs to the PM and cabinet.
  • Opens Parliament each year with the Queen's Speech (the government's plans); all Acts of Parliament are made in her name.
  • Receives foreign ambassadors, hosts visiting heads of state, makes state visits — the focus for national identity and pride, stability while governments change.

House of Commons

  • The more important chamber — because its members are democratically elected.
  • Each MP represents a parliamentary constituency.
  • The PM and almost all cabinet members are MPs.
  • MPs: represent everyone in their constituency, help create new laws, scrutinise and question government, debate the big issues.
  • The Speaker — chairs debates, is neutral despite being an MP, is chosen by other MPs in a secret ballot, keeps order, guarantees the opposition debate time, represents Parliament on ceremonial occasions.

House of Lords

  • Members are peers — not elected, no constituency.
  • Until 1958: hereditary title-holders, senior judges, Church of England bishops.
  • Since 1958: life peers — nominated by the PM (also other party leaders and an independent Appointments Commission), appointed by the monarch, for their lifetime.
  • Since 1999: hereditary peers lost the automatic right to attend — they now elect a few representatives.
  • Role: more independent than the Commons; proposes amendments, checks laws, holds government to account, brings specialist knowledge.
  • The Commons can overrule the Lords — but rarely does.

Elections

  • General elections at least every five years; the party with a majority of constituencies forms the government — no majority may mean a coalition.
  • By-election — held when an MP dies or resigns.
  • Voting system: first past the post — most votes in the constituency wins the seat.
  • European Parliament elections: every five years, by proportional representation; members are MEPs.
The National Anthem — first verse (learn it; it gets quoted)
“God save our gracious Queen! Long live our noble Queen! God save the Queen! Send her victorious, happy and glorious, long to reign over us, God save the Queen!”

New citizens swear (oath) or affirm loyalty at the citizenship ceremony: “…I will be faithful and bear true allegiance to Her Majesty Queen Elizabeth the Second, her Heirs and Successors, according to law.”

Memory hook 1958 gives life · 1999 takes inheritance Lords reform in two beats: 1958 — life peers arrive (PM nominates, monarch appoints). 1999 — hereditary peers lose their automatic seats. And the monarch's three verbs: advise, warn, encourage — never “decide”.
Exam trap The constitution is unwritten because Britain never had a revolution — not because nobody wrote it down. First past the post elects MPs; proportional representation elects MEPs (and the devolved bodies). The Speaker is neutral and chosen by secret ballot of MPs — not appointed by the PM or the Queen.

PM, cabinet & opposition

  • The Prime Minister — leader of the party in power; appoints the cabinet and controls important public appointments; lives at 10 Downing Street with a country house, Chequers; can be changed by party MPs or by resignation (usually after losing an election).
  • Cabinet — about 20 senior MPs made ministers in charge of departments; meets weekly; big decisions often need Parliament.
  • Chancellor of the Exchequer — the economy · Home Secretary — crime, policing, immigration · Foreign Secretary — relations with foreign countries · other Secretaries of State (education, health, defence). Departments also have Ministers of State and Parliamentary Under-Secretaries.
  • The opposition — the second-largest party; its leader challenges the PM at Prime Minister's Questions every sitting week and appoints a shadow cabinet.

Parties, civil service & councils

  • Main parties: Conservative, Labour, Liberal Democrats, plus parties for Scottish, Welsh and Northern Irish interests; independents stand on local issues.
  • Pressure and lobby groups try to influence policy — e.g. the CBI (business), Greenpeace (environment), Liberty (human rights).
  • Civil servants — deliver and develop policy, accountable to ministers, chosen on merit, politically neutral; values: integrity, honesty, objectivity, impartiality.
  • Local authorities — elected councils funded by central government and local taxes; some areas have district and county councils; many appoint a ceremonial mayor; London has 33 local authorities plus the Greater London Authority and Mayor of London; council elections are held in May.

Devolved administrations — since 1997

  • Powers stay in Westminster for defence, foreign affairs, immigration, taxation and social security — the devolved bodies run much of the rest, including education.
  • Wales — the National Assembly in Cardiff: 60 Assembly Members (AMs), elected every four years by proportional representation; works in Welsh and English; since 2011 it can pass laws in 20 areas without the UK Parliament's agreement.
  • Scotland — the Scottish Parliament (est. 1999) at Holyrood, Edinburgh: 129 MSPs, elected by proportional representation; legislates on everything not reserved, including civil and criminal law, health, education, planning — and can raise taxes.
  • Northern Ireland — an earlier NI Parliament (1922) was abolished in 1972; today's Assembly followed the Belfast (Good Friday) Agreement, 1998: 108 MLAs at Stormont, elected by proportional representation, governing by power-sharing; the UK government can suspend it — it has run continuously since 2007.

The media & government

  • Parliamentary proceedings are broadcast on TV and published in Hansard (in large libraries and at www.parliament.uk).
  • The UK has a free press — newspapers are free of government control (owners and editors may campaign).
  • Radio and TV coverage must be balanced — rival viewpoints get equal time.

Who votes, who stands

  • Voting age: 18, set in 1969.
  • Can vote: UK-born and naturalised citizens, plus resident Commonwealth and Irish citizens; resident EU citizens can vote in all elections EXCEPT general elections.
  • Electoral register — register via your council's electoral registration office (find it at www.aboutmyvote.co.uk); a form goes to every household each September/October; Northern Ireland registers individually; the register can be viewed under supervision.
  • Polling stations (polling places in Scotland) open 7 am–10 pm; you get a polling card; NI voters need photo ID; postal voting is available.
  • Standing for office — 18+ and a UK, Irish or Commonwealth citizen; exceptions include armed forces, civil servants and people with certain convictions; Lords members can't stand for the Commons.
  • Visiting: public galleries in both Houses — write to your MP for tickets or queue (Commons 1–2 hours; Lords easier). Holyrood, the Senedd (Cardiff Bay) and Stormont all take visitors too.
Memory hook The devolution phone number: 60 – 129 – 108 West to east then across the water: Wales 60, Scotland 129, NI 108. Scotland got its parliament in 1999, NI's deal was 1998 (Good Friday), Wales levelled up in 2011 (laws in 20 areas). Westminster keeps D-F-I-T-S: defence, foreign affairs, immigration, taxation, social security.
Exam trap EU citizens vote in local and devolved elections but NOT general elections — Commonwealth and Irish citizens can vote in all. The Home Secretary handles immigration (not the Foreign Secretary); the Chancellor handles the economy. Only Scotland's parliament can raise taxes among the devolved bodies named here.

The EU story in one line each

  • 25 March 1957 — six countries sign the Treaty of Rome: Belgium, France, Germany, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands — creating the EEC.

  • Today: 27 member states; EU law is legally binding in the UK and every member state.
Memory hook Descending staircase: 190+ → 54 → 47 → 27 → 15 → 5 UN members → Commonwealth → Council of Europe → EU → UN Security Council seats → its permanent members (UK included). Six numbers, biggest to smallest — recite it as one breath.
Exam trap The Council of Europe is NOT the EU — it has 47 members, makes no laws, and wrote the European Convention on Human Rights. The Commonwealth has no power over members (it can only suspend). Rome was signed in 1957 without the UK; Britain arrived 16 years later, in 1973.

Respecting the Law

Criminal vs civil · police · courts · juries

Knowing and obeying the law is a core responsibility of every resident — breaking it can cost you settlement. The law applies equally to everyone, police and government included.

Criminal law — investigated & punished

  • Carrying a weapon of any kind — even for self-defence — is an offence.
  • Drugs: selling or buying heroin, cocaine, ecstasy, cannabis.
  • Racial crime: causing harassment, alarm or distress because of religion or ethnic origin.
  • Selling tobacco or alcohol to under-18s; smoking in nearly every enclosed public place.
  • Drinking in alcohol-free zones — police can confiscate or fine.

Civil law — disputes between people

  • Housing — landlord–tenant disputes.
  • Consumer rights — faulty goods or services.
  • Employment — wages, unfair dismissal, discrimination.
  • Debt — money owed.

The police

  • Three duties: protect life and property · keep the peace · prevent and detect crime.
  • Forces are independent of government, led by Chief Constables.
  • Police and Crime Commissioners (PCCs) — first elected November 2012 in England & Wales; they set local priorities and budgets and appoint Chief Constables.
  • Officers must obey the law themselves — no misuse of authority, false statements, rudeness or racial discrimination.
  • PCSOs (community support officers) patrol, work with the public, support officers at crime scenes.
  • Complaints go to the station or Chief Constable — or the Independent Police Complaints Commission (E&W), Police Complaints Commissioner (Scotland), Police Ombudsman (NI).
  • Terror threats: Al Qa'ida and like-minded groups; Northern Ireland-related terrorism — report extremist recruiters to your local police.

Judges & the courts

  • Judges interpret the law and keep trials fair — government cannot interfere; if the government acts illegally, it must change its policies or ask Parliament to change the law.
  • Minor crime: Magistrates' Courts (E, W, NI) / Justice of the Peace Courts (Scotland). Magistrates and JPs are unpaid, need no legal qualifications; NI uses paid, legally qualified District Judges.
  • Serious crime: Crown Court (E, W, NI) / Sheriff Court (Scotland) — judge and jury.
  • Jury: 12 members (E, W, NI), 15 in Scotland; verdicts: guilty / not guilty — Scotland adds “not proven”. Jury service is compulsory for those summoned, unless ineligible or excused for good reason.
  • Youth Court — ages 10–17, up to three specially trained magistrates; parents must attend; public excluded; the young person cannot be named or photographed. Scotland: Children's Hearings; NI: youth conferencing.
  • Civil cases: County Courts (Sheriff Court in Scotland); big cases go to the High Court (Court of Session in Scotland).
  • Small claims — informal, in an ordinary room before a judge: under £10,000 in England & Wales, under £5,000 in Scotland & NI — Scotland calls it the simple procedure; claims can start at Money Claims Online.
  • Solicitors — trained lawyers who advise, act and represent; fees usually follow time spent.
Memory hook Scotland does everything a bit differently One rule of thumb saves eight facts: in Scotland it's JP Courts (not magistrates'), the Sheriff Court (not Crown/County), 15 jurors (not 12), three verdicts (“not proven”), a £5,000 small-claims limit (England & Wales allow £10,000), and Children's Hearings for the young.
Exam trap Carrying a weapon is criminal “even if it is for self-defence” — the handbook's exact bait. Magistrates are unpaid and unqualified, but NI's District Judges are paid and legally qualified. PCCs are elected by the public and appoint Chief Constables — not the other way round.

Fundamental Principles

Rights · protection · tax · driving

Britain's rights tradition runs from Magna Carta through the Habeas Corpus Act to the Bill of Rights of 1689. British experts helped draft the European Convention on Human Rights, which the UK signed in 1950 — and the Human Rights Act 1998 wrote it into UK law.

The Convention rights to list

  • Right to life
  • Prohibition of torture
  • Prohibition of slavery and forced labour
  • Right to liberty and security
  • Right to a fair trial
  • Freedom of thought, conscience and religion
  • Freedom of expression (speech)

Equality & protection

  • Discrimination is unlawful on grounds of age, disability, sex, pregnancy and maternity, race, religion or belief, sexuality or marital status. Help: Citizens Advice Bureau, the Equality and Human Rights Commission and NI/Scotland equivalents.
  • Domestic violence is a serious crime — including forced sexual relations; 24-hour National Domestic Violence Freephone Helpline: 0808 2000 247; refuges and shelters exist.
  • Female genital mutilation (FGM) is illegal — and so is taking someone abroad for it.
  • Arranged marriage (both parties agree) is acceptable; forced marriage (no free consent) is a crime. Forced Marriage Protection Orders since 2008 (E, W, NI; Scotland Nov 2011); breaching one: up to two years in prison.

Income tax & National Insurance

  • Income tax applies to wages, self-employed profits, taxable benefits, pensions, property, savings, dividends.
  • It pays for roads, education, police and the armed forces.
  • Employees pay via PAYE (Pay As You Earn); the self-employed file self-assessment with HMRC.
  • Almost everyone in paid work — including the self-employed — pays National Insurance Contributions, which fund state benefits and the NHS (e.g. the state retirement pension). Too few contributions → possibly no Jobseeker's Allowance or full pension.
  • Your National Insurance number — a unique personal account number, given just before age 16; non-UK nationals get one via the DWP, proving identity and the right to work.

Driving — the rulebook

  • Drive a car or motorcycle at 17; a moped at 16.
  • Pass a theory test (multiple choice + hazard perception) and a practical test.
  • Licence valid until age 70 — then renew every three years.
  • Northern Ireland: newly qualified drivers show an R (restricted) plate for one year.
  • Foreign licences: EU / Iceland / Liechtenstein / Norway — valid as long as the licence is; all others — 12 months, then you need a UK licence.
  • Your car must be registered with the DVLA, have road tax (disc displayed), insurance — driving uninsured is a serious criminal offence — and an annual MOT test if over three years old.
Memory hook Rights timeline: 1689 → 1950 → 1998 Bill of Rights (1689) starts the tradition, the UK signs the ECHR in 1950, the Human Rights Act 1998 brings it home. Driving ladder: 16 moped → 17 car → 70 renewals begin → every 3 years after.
Exam trap Arranged ≠ forced: arranged marriage is legal; forced marriage is criminal. Income tax builds roads and schools; National Insurance funds the NHS and state pension — different taxes, different jobs. The MOT is for cars over three years old, and it's annual.

The shared values

  • Obey and respect the law; be aware of and respect others' rights; treat others with fairness; behave responsibly.
  • Help and protect your family; respect and preserve the environment.
  • Treat everyone equally; work to support yourself and your family; help others; vote in local and national elections.
  • Being a good neighbour: introduce yourself, respect privacy, keep noise down, tend your garden, put refuse out on collection days.

Civic duties & schools

  • Jury service — anyone on the electoral register aged 18–70 can be randomly selected.
  • Help in schools: listen to children read, help in class, support sports days, fundraise via the PTA.
  • Become a school governor (a school board member in Scotland) at 18+ — no upper age limit: set strategic direction, ensure accountability, monitor performance; apply direct to a school or via the School Governors' One-Stop Shop.
  • Support a party: canvassing; stand for office if you're a British, Irish or eligible Commonwealth citizen (EU citizens: some non-MP roles).

Giving — blood, organs, time

  • Giving blood takes about an hour; separate registration services for England & North Wales, Wales, Scotland, NI.
  • Organ donation — join the NHS register; living donors can give a kidney.
  • Volunteer with local services: hospitals, youth projects, special constables, lay magistrates.
  • Charities to recognise: British Red Cross, Age UK, NSPCC, Crisis, Shelter, Cancer Research UK, National Trust, Friends of the Earth, PDSA.
  • National Citizen Service — for 16–17-year-olds: outdoor activities and community projects.
  • Environment: recycle (less energy, less landfill), shop locally, walk or take public transport.
Memory hook Jury 18–70 · governor 18–∞ · NCS 16–17 Three age brackets carry this section. Jury duty has a ceiling (70); school governing doesn't; the NCS is the teenagers' door in.
Exam trap Jury selection comes from the electoral register — another reason registering matters. Match the charity to its cause: NSPCC — children, Age UK — older people, Shelter/Crisis — homelessness, PDSA — animals, Friends of the Earth — environment.
